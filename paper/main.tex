\title{Predicting Benchmark Scores from Unpaired Model Evaluations}
\author{Anonymous}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Evaluating a generative model on all available benchmarks is prohibitively expensive. As
such, models are often evaluated on different subsets of tasks and/or different subsets of
queries from a given task, and the resulting (model $\times$ task) score matrix is sparse
and noisy. In this paper, we introduce the \textbf{Product Kernel Perspective Space
(\PKPS)}---a collection of low-dimensional representations of models based on their
embedded responses---that generalizes existing benchmark-prediction methods to the sparse
and noisy evaluation regime. Theoretically, we show that \PKPS more effectively uses information from unpaired
queries for a given task. Empirically, we show that \PKPS outperforms sample means,
item-response theory, and paired methods where evaluation is sparse, noisy, or unpaired,
and that it is a complementary signal to score-based methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Generative models are evaluated on an ever-growing collection of benchmarks spanning
knowledge, reasoning, code, translation, and safety \citep{helm}. Evaluating a model on
all of them is prohibitively expensive. As a result, models are evaluated on different
subsets of tasks and on different subsets of queries within a task: a recent compilation of $84$ frontier models on $133$ benchmarks
finds only $23\%$ of the score matrix filled \citep{benchpress}. Nor is this an accident
of curation---it is how modern evaluation works. Arena-style platforms score each model on
whatever conversations its users happen to have \citep{chiang2024chatbot};
contamination-aware benchmarks refresh their items, so models evaluated in different
months answer different questions \citep{white2024livebench}; and cost-driven subsampling
gives each evaluation run its own item subset \citep{perlitz2024efficient}. The resulting
(model $\times$ task) score matrix is sparse, its entries are noisy averages over few
queries, and the responses beneath it are \emph{unpaired}: two models rarely answer the
same queries. Predicting the missing and true entries of this matrix would let
practitioners reuse cached evaluations instead of paying to re-run them
\citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks, vivek2024anchor, benchpress}.

Existing predictors use each cell's \emph{aggregate} score: low-rank matrix completion
imputes missing cells \citep{benchpress}, and item-response theory extrapolates a full
score from a scored item subset \citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks}. But the responses that
produced those scores carry more information than the scores themselves. The Data Kernel
Perspective Space (DKPS) \citep{dkps_qeff} uses them: it embeds each model from its query
responses via classical multidimensional scaling and regresses benchmark scores in that
space, query-efficiently. However, DKPS's distance between two models is the average
difference of their responses \emph{to the same queries}---a \emph{paired} design. On
unpaired data it has nothing to compare, and the information in the unpaired responses is
thrown away.

In this paper, we introduce the \textbf{Product Kernel Perspective Space (\PKPS)}, a
collection of low-dimensional representations of models based on their embedded responses.
\PKPS compares responses to \emph{similar}---rather than only identical---queries, so
every unpaired response contributes and paired DKPS is recovered as a special case
(Section~\ref{sec:method}). Theoretically, we show that the query budget \PKPS requires
to reach a target accuracy does not depend on the fraction of queries shared across models
(Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}). Empirically, we demonstrate on two heterogeneous suites---$93$
models on HELM tasks and $45$ modern models from the Every Eval Ever datastore
\citep{eee2026}---that \PKPS achieves lower error than sample means, item-response
theory, and DKPS in sparse, noisy, and unpaired regimes, matching the accuracy of sample
means with an $8\times$ smaller query budget, and that ensembling it with score-based
methods reduces the error of low-rank completion by up to $28\%$
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\subsection{Problem setting and notation}
\label{sec:setting}
A \emph{model} is a random mapping $f:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{X}$ from a query space to a
response space; querying $f$ at $q$ returns a draw from the distribution $f(q)$. We
observe $n$ models $f_1,\dots,f_n$, each run on its own \emph{query set}
$Q_i\subseteq\mathcal{Q}$; the sets may overlap arbitrarily, and the paired design
$Q_1=\dots=Q_n$ is a special case. Two fixed embedding functions map raw objects to
vectors: a \emph{response} embedding $\mathrm{embed}_R:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}$ and
a \emph{query} embedding $\mathrm{embed}_Q:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{p'}$. For
$q_j\in Q_i$ we write
$x_{ij}=\tfrac1r\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mathrm{embed}_R\!\big(f_i(q_j)^{(k)}\big)$ for the
average of the $r$ sampled response embeddings and $u_j=\mathrm{embed}_Q(q_j)$ for the
embedded query.

Queries are grouped into $T$ tasks. Model $i$'s \emph{true score} on task $t$, $y_{it}$,
is its mean score over the task's full query set; running the model on $m_{it}\ge0$ of
those queries yields an observed \emph{sample score} whose noise scales as
$1/\sqrt{m_{it}}$. The goal is to predict $y_{it}$ for every cell of the
(model $\times$ task) matrix: cells observed with few queries ($m_{it}>0$;
\emph{denoising}, the efficient-benchmarking end) and cells never run ($m_{it}=0$;
\emph{completion}). We write $y_i$ when the benchmark has a single score. Two properties
of the design recur throughout: \emph{coverage}, the fraction of cells with $m_{it}>0$,
and \emph{pairing}, the fraction $\rho$ of a cell's queries shared across models; real
evaluations sit near $\rho\approx0$.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Low-dimensional representations of models.} Some methods compare models through
their internals: activations on shared inputs \citep{duderstadt2023comparing,
huh2024platonic, horwitz2025atlas} or weights \citep{chen2025weights}. Evaluation, however,
is black-box: it sees responses, not internals. The Data Kernel Perspective Space (DKPS)
embeds models from their responses via multidimensional scaling \citep{torgerson1952mds,
helm2024tracking}; the representations are consistent and concentrate
\citep{acharyya2024consistent, acharyya2025concentration}, support statistical inference
\citep{helm2025inference}, and are query-efficient \citep{dkps_qeff}. But DKPS averages
over responses to \emph{shared} queries---a paired design. \PKPS keeps the response-level
representation and replaces identity matching with a product kernel, so models that answer
different queries remain comparable.

\paragraph{Benchmark prediction.} A second line predicts unobserved (model, task) scores so that
not every evaluation must be run; efficient benchmarking and matrix completion are its two ends.
Efficient benchmarking scores a small item subset and extrapolates. Item-response theory selects
items by difficulty and discrimination \citep{rasch1960, polo2024tinybenchmarks, metabench};
anchor points cluster items by cross-model agreement \citep{vivek2024anchor}; cognitive-scale
embeddings \citep{bean2025scales} and reinforcement learning \citep{li2024active} select items
adaptively; and design analyses optimize the cost--reliability tradeoff
\citep{perlitz2024efficient}. Closer to us, DKPS predicts a held-out score from cached responses
\citep{dkps_qeff}, datamodels predict outputs from inputs \citep{ilyas2022datamodels}, and
lifelong benchmarks reuse past evaluations \citep{prabhu2024lifelong}. Matrix completion instead
predicts cells that were never run, exploiting low rank across the score matrix
\citep{candes2009exact, mazumder2010softimpute, koren2009matrix}; BenchPress finds it is
approximately rank-two and completes it in logit space \citep{benchpress}. These methods use only
each cell's \emph{aggregate} score, not its responses. \PKPS unifies the two ends---a cell with
$m\ge0$ queries spans efficient benchmarking ($m{>}0$) and completion ($m{=}0$)---and adds an
item-level embedding signal that we ensemble with matrix completion.

\section{The Product Kernel Perspective Space}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Perspective space.} Each method summarizes the $n$ models of
Section~\ref{sec:setting} in an $n\times n$ affinity matrix $A$ (defined below). The squared distance between two models is
$D^2(a,b)=A_{aa}+A_{bb}-2A_{ab}$ (clamped at $0$), and their $d$-dimensional
\emph{representations} are the classical-multidimensional-scaling minimizer
\begin{equation}
(\hat\psi_1,\dots,\hat\psi_n)=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{z_1,\dots,z_n\in\mathbb{R}^d}\
\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\big(\|z_i-z_k\|-D(i,k)\big)^2,
\label{eq:mds}
\end{equation}
so model $i$ is the point $\hat\psi_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$, its \emph{perspective}. Paired DKPS
and \PKPS differ only in the affinity $A$.

\paragraph{Paired DKPS.} With all $n$ models answering the same $m$ queries
($Q_1=\dots=Q_n$), DKPS uses the identity-matched affinity
$A_{ab}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_j k_R(x_{aj},x_{bj})$: response $j$ of model $a$ is paired only
with response $j$ of model $b$, so the design must be paired.

\paragraph{Product kernel.} \PKPS replaces identity matching with a query kernel $k_Q$:
\begin{equation}
A_{ab} = \frac{\sum_{j\in Q_a}\sum_{l\in Q_b} k_Q(q_j,q_l)\, k_R(x_{aj},x_{bl})}
              {\sum_{j\in Q_a}\sum_{l\in Q_b} k_Q(q_j,q_l)},
\label{eq:A}
\end{equation}
where $Q_a$ is the query set of model $a$. Each term is normalized by its own query-kernel
mass, so models that answered different numbers of (or entirely different) queries are
still comparable; responses to \emph{similar} queries---even across tasks---contribute and
unpaired evaluations are used (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}). Paired DKPS is the special case
$k_Q=\delta$, where only $j=l$ survives.

\paragraph{Kernels.} The response kernel $k_R$ compares embedded responses, the query
kernel $k_Q$ compares embedded queries. Paired DKPS uses a linear response kernel and the
identity query kernel $k_Q=\delta$. We use an RBF query kernel over the embedded queries,
$k_Q(q_j,q_l)=\exp(-\|u_j-u_l\|^2/2\sigma^2)$, with bandwidth $\sigma$ chosen per run
(below); $k_R$ is RBF in the synthetic study and linear on real data, where linearity makes
the kernel factorize across response domains (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Since Eq.~\ref{eq:A}
normalizes each pair by its own kernel mass, $A$ need not be positive semidefinite;
classical MDS embeds the doubly-centered matrix by its top-$d$ nonnegative eigenvalues, as
is standard. $A_{ab}$ costs $|Q_a||Q_b|$ kernel terms per pair, and one response-kernel
pass serves the whole bandwidth grid.

\subsection{Theoretical properties of the \PKPS}
\PKPS is a strict generalization of DKPS, in two senses. \emph{DKPS limit:} with
$k_Q=\delta$ only equal queries lying in both query sets survive the double sum of
Eq.~\ref{eq:A}, so it reduces to paired DKPS over the shared queries,
$A_{ab}=\tfrac{1}{|Q_a\cap Q_b|}\sum_{q\in Q_a\cap Q_b}k_R(x_{aq},x_{bq})$; and since the
RBF query kernel converges pointwise to $\delta$ as $\sigma\!\to\!0$ (distinct queries
having distinct embeddings), DKPS is also the small-bandwidth limit. \emph{Unpaired
comparability:} for any strictly positive $k_Q$ the normalizer is positive for any pair of
nonempty query sets, so $A_{ab}$ is defined even on disjoint sets, where paired DKPS is
undefined. Between the two limits, $\sigma$ smoothly trades exact matching for
cross-query bridging.

\subsection{Inference in the \PKPS}
Inference is a two-step statistical problem, exactly as in the DKPS \citep{dkps_qeff}:
embed each model into the perspective space by the scaling of Eq.~\ref{eq:mds}, then
regress its benchmark score on the resulting coordinate. Concretely, on a set of reference
models with known scores $\{(f_i,y_i)\}$ we fit a decision function
$\hat h:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathcal{Y}$ on the pairs $\{(\hat\psi_i,y_i)\}$ and predict any
model's score as $\hat y(f)=\hat h(\hat\psi(f))$. The only free choices are the
query-kernel bandwidth $\sigma$ and the regressor $\hat h$, which we describe next; we then
add the score channel that turns the embedding into an ensemble.

\paragraph{Choosing the bandwidth.} The optimal $\sigma$ depends on how paired the data
is: small when query overlap is high (exact matching), large when it is low (bridging). We
set it per run by cross-validation over a median-scaled grid
$\sigma\in\{0.03,\dots,3\}\cdot\mathrm{med}$ (median pairwise query distance), selecting
the $\sigma$ whose perspective best predicts the \emph{observed} scores under
leave-one-model-out $k$-NN---never the held-out scores we report, so the choice is not
circular. Because the grid reaches near-delta, cross-validation \emph{recovers DKPS} when
overlap is high and bridges only when it must.

\paragraph{Estimation pipeline.} For each replicate we (i) PCA-reduce response and query
embeddings on the \emph{observed} rows only (dimension by the second Zhu--Ghodsi elbow);
(ii) form $D$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:A}; (iii) embed models by classical MDS; (iv) map the
embedding to scores with the \emph{same} head as the matrix-completion baseline up to the
factor model---logit transform, per-task standardization, two-way (model $+$ task)
bias---then $k$-NN regression of the residual on the embedding, predicting every cell
(held-out cells directly, observed cells leave-one-out). Matching the per-task
standardization matters: without it the model offset is dominated by the
highest-logit-variance tasks, biasing the embedding on multi-scale suites.

\paragraph{The embedding-$\oplus$-score ensemble.} Each cell combines the embedding with
whichever score signal carries independent information. On a \emph{missing} cell that is
matrix completion \citep{benchpress}, blended with \PKPS by a weight fit by held-out
cross-validation on observed scores. On an \emph{observed} cell it is the cell's own
sample mean, shrunk toward the \PKPS estimate by the precision weight
$\sigma_q^2/(\sigma_q^2+e_{\PKPS}^2)$---sample noise variance over total disagreement with
\PKPS---so the sample dominates as queries grow and the embedding as noise grows. Both
quantities are read off observed queries ($\hat p(1-\hat p)/m$; depth-weighted
disagreement), so the weight never touches held-out scores and the score baselines see
exactly the same information.

\subsection{Query efficiency}
\label{sec:qeff}
\PKPS is designed to reach a target prediction accuracy from fewer evaluated queries than
paired DKPS: by bridging responses to similar queries it uses evidence that DKPS, limited
to shared queries, discards. We formalize this by extending the machinery of
\citet{dkps_qeff}.
\begin{definition}[Population \PKPS]
\label{def:pop}
Let each model $a$'s (query, response) pairs be drawn i.i.d.\ from a design distribution
$P_a$. The \emph{population affinity} is
$A^*_{ab}=\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')\,k_R(x_a,x_b)]\,/\,\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')]$ with draws from
$P_a$ and $P_b$ independent, the population squared distances are
$D^{*2}(a,b)=A^*_{aa}+A^*_{bb}-2A^*_{ab}$, and the \emph{population perspectives}
$\psi_1,\dots,\psi_n\in\mathbb{R}^d$ are the rank-$d$ spectral embedding of the centered
Gram matrix $B^*$ of $D^{*2}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{assumption}[Score regularity]\label{ass:score}
The benchmark score is bounded and $\gamma$-Lipschitz on the population perspective space.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Model support]\label{ass:support}
The model distribution places mass on every neighborhood of the population perspective
space.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Kernel and geometry regularity]\label{ass:reg}
Kernels and embedded responses are bounded; the \emph{cross-model query-kernel mass} is
bounded below, $\kappa=\inf_{a,b}\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')]>0$; and $B^*$ has rank $d$ with
spectral gap $\lambda_d>0$. ($\kappa$ is the population analogue of $\rho$: positive for
an RBF $k_Q$ whenever query \emph{distributions} overlap, even with no shared query.)
\end{assumption}
\begin{proposition}[Concentration of \PKPS perspectives]
\label{prop:conc}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:reg}, with at least $m$ queries per model, for any
$\delta\in(0,1)$, with probability at least $1-\delta$ there is an orthogonal $W$ such
that
$\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le
C\kappa^{-2}\lambda_d^{-1/2}\sqrt{d}\,n\sqrt{\log(4n^2/\delta)/2m}$,
where $C$ depends only on the kernel bounds (proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}).
\end{proposition}
\begin{definition}[Query efficiency; following \citealp{dkps_qeff}]
\label{def:qeff}
For a score estimator $h$ and a query design with per-model budget $m$, let
$m_h(\epsilon)$ be the smallest budget at which $h$ attains
$\mathrm{MSE}\le\epsilon$ with high probability for some number of reference models.
Estimator $h$ is \emph{query-efficient relative to} $h'$ if
$m_h(\epsilon)\le m_{h'}(\epsilon)$ for all sufficiently small $\epsilon$.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}[Query efficiency of \PKPS]
\label{thm:qeff}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:score}--\ref{ass:reg}, for any $\epsilon>0$ there exist
$(n,m)$, with $m$ polynomial in $(1/\epsilon,1/\kappa,n)$ and independent of the pairing
fraction $\rho$, such that nearest-neighbor regression in the \PKPS attains mean squared
error at most $\epsilon$ with high probability; in particular $m_{\PKPS}(\epsilon)$ does
not grow as $\rho\to0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Proposition~\ref{prop:conc} bounds the estimation error of every perspective. By
Assumption~\ref{ass:score} and the triangle inequality, the nearest-neighbor regression
error splits into two estimation terms and the population nearest-neighbor distance, which
Assumption~\ref{ass:support} makes small for $n$ large; choosing
$m=\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\kappa,1/\epsilon)$ gives $\mathrm{MSE}\le\epsilon$, and no step
involves $\rho$. Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}.
\end{proof}
Applied to paired DKPS ($k_Q=\delta$), the same argument retains only the $\rho M_{ij}$
shared query pairs, so the DKPS bound's effective budget is $\rho m$ and its guarantee
degrades as $\rho\to0$ while Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} does not. In the sense of
Definition~\ref{def:qeff} we conjecture strict separation; Section~\ref{sec:results} is
its empirical counterpart.
\begin{conjecture}[Strict gap]
\label{conj:qeff}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:score}--\ref{ass:reg}, \PKPS is query-efficient relative to
paired DKPS, with $m_{\mathrm{DKPS}}(\epsilon)/m_{\PKPS}(\epsilon)$ increasing as
$\rho\to0$; the two coincide in the fully paired limit $\rho=1$.
\end{conjecture}

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
We evaluate \PKPS on synthetic data, where every lever of the observation process can be
swept, and on two heterogeneous benchmark suites. Throughout, the two prediction problems
of Section~\ref{sec:setting} structure the experiments: denoising observed cells (query
efficiency) and predicting missing ones (completion).

\subsection{Evaluation setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Synthetic data.} We generate $n$ models with latent ability vectors $v_i$ and
$T$ tasks with latent vectors $t_k$, scores $v_i\cdot t_k+\epsilon$, and queries drawn
near each task center. Each model answers $M_{ij}$ queries per task, of which $m_{ii'}$
are shared with another model (overlap $\rho=m_{ii'}/M_{ij}$); a task is observed with
probability $p_{\mathrm{task}}$ and a query with probability $p_{\mathrm{query}}$.

\paragraph{Benchmark suites.} The first suite is $18$ tasks across four HELM datasets
with very different response types---MATH ($7$ math subjects), WMT-14 ($5$ translation
directions), MedQA (medical multiple-choice), and LegalBench ($5$ legal classification
subsets)---on the $93$ models evaluated on all four. MATH and WMT have rich free-text
responses; MedQA and LegalBench responses are single tokens for which text embeddings are
degenerate, so we encode each dataset's responses natively (Gemini embeddings vs.\ one-hot
answers) in disjoint blocks. With a linear $k_R$ this makes cross-dataset response
similarity exactly zero, and a single shared query embedding with a within-domain
bandwidth keeps $k_Q$ near zero across domains: the product kernel factorizes by domain,
never comparing a proof to a legal label. The second suite, from the Every Eval Ever
datastore \citep{eee2026}, is $45$ modern models on $16$ tasks (MATH-MC levels $1$--$5$,
GSM-MC, GPQA-Diamond, and nine judging categories from JudgeBench and RewardBench~2), with
per-cell query pools drawn independently per model ($\sim\!10\%$ incidental
overlap)---unpaired by construction (Appendix~\ref{app:eee}).

\paragraph{Prediction methods.} We compare six predictors. The \emph{sample mean} is a
cell's own $m$-query average. \emph{IRT} fits a one-parameter logistic model on the
largest shared item block \citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks}. \emph{Matrix completion} is the
logit-space low-rank imputation of \citet{benchpress} (missing cells only). \emph{DKPS}
and \emph{\PKPS} regress scores on the model embeddings of Section~\ref{sec:method},
DKPS being the identity-query-kernel limit. The \emph{ensemble} combines \PKPS with
whichever score signal a cell has (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\paragraph{Measurement.} Every experiment predicts held-out or true cell scores and
reports MAE against the full-evaluation score, mean $\pm1$ SEM over $16$ seeds ($50$ on
synthetic data). Sweeps vary one observation lever at a time---queries per cell $m$
(noise), task coverage $p_{\mathrm{task}}$, cohort size $n$, and, on synthetic data, the
pairing $\rho$---holding the others fixed (grids in Appendix~\ref{app:details}); all
hyperparameters, including the query bandwidth, are chosen per run by the leak-free
cross-validation of Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{Synthetic study.} We predict held-out (model, task) scores, comparing \PKPS
(RBF query kernel) against DKPS (identity) with the score-only sample baseline as a floor
(Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic}).
\PKPS---selecting its bandwidth by the same leak-free cross-validation as on real
data---beats DKPS on \emph{every} lever, and both beat the sample floor: bridging similar
queries (across tasks as well as within) supplies signal exact-match DKPS cannot reach, so
the margin is wide even when mostly paired (a--c). The gap peaks where pairing is scarce:
as $p_{\mathrm{query}}$ drops (d) DKPS degrades while \PKPS stays flat, and sweeping
$\rho$ directly (e), DKPS collapses toward the floor as $\rho\!\to\!0$ while \PKPS is
flat---it does not rely on pairing.

Panel (f) is the \emph{denoising} form of the same problem: each cell is now \emph{observed}
with $M_{ij}$ noisy queries (sample noise $\propto1/\sqrt{M_{ij}}$) and we predict its true
score. The raw sample mean (gray) falls like $1/\sqrt{M_{ij}}$; smoothing those noisy scores
over the perspective denoises well below it while measurement noise dominates---\PKPS reaches
at a single query the accuracy the raw sample needs roughly five queries to match, and does
so whether queries are paired or not, while DKPS denoises only when paired (unpaired DKPS,
dashed, barely improves). Only once the budget is large is the raw sample precise enough to
overtake the embedding's residual bias. This is the empirical counterpart of
Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} and Conjecture~\ref{conj:qeff}.

\paragraph{Query efficiency.} Both real-data applications are inherently
\emph{unpaired}---each model is run on its own queries, the real-data counterpart of the
$\rho\!\to\!0$ limit above. Because the embedding captures response
\emph{style}, which is stable even when the per-cell sample mean is noisy, it denoises
cheap evaluations. Each model answers $m$ queries per cell, sampled independently---itself
an unpaired regime, on which IRT is poorly identified; we predict the true full-eval score
(Figure~\ref{fig:qe}). At a small budget the embedding is far more accurate than the
raw mean: at $m{=}1$, \PKPS scores $0.136$ and the ensemble $0.133$ versus the sample's
$0.292$; \PKPS at one query roughly matches the sample at four. As the budget grows the
raw mean catches up and eventually overtakes the embedding's residual bias ($0.034$ vs.\
$0.088$ at $m{=}32$); the leak-free ensemble tracks the better channel to within a few
thousandths. The advantage is robust across cohort size and task coverage (panels b, c).
Per cell, the denoising win is broad but tail-driven: the ensemble beats the raw sample on
most cells, with the largest gains at the smallest budgets and on the rich-response datasets

\section{Discussion}
Real leaderboards are organic: each model answers whatever queries its authors chose, so
the responses benchmark estimation must compare are \emph{unpaired}---and methods that
need a shared block of identical queries collapse there. \PKPS removes that requirement
with a product kernel comparing responses to \emph{similar} queries, recovering paired
DKPS in its $\sigma\!\to\!0$ limit and scaling to heterogeneous suites through a
block-diagonal kernel. Our central finding is that benchmark estimation survives unpaired evaluation: as
$\rho\!\to\!0$, DKPS and IRT degrade sharply while \PKPS holds.
That capability yields two applications from one embedding---denoising cheap, few-query
evaluations and predicting never-run cells---and the ensemble with a cell's own sample is
never the worse choice. None of this assumes pairing or exhaustive evaluation.

\paragraph{Limitations.} \PKPS embeds every observed response and query; cost and quality
inherit from the embedders, which we do not ablate. Our block-diagonal instantiation
disables cross-domain bridging; whether a shared response space would help is open. The
affinity is quadratic in queries per model pair (approximation needed only for very large
logs). Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} is existential
(Assumption~\ref{ass:support} gives no rate in $n$), and the strict separation from paired DKPS remains a
conjecture. Finally, per-cell wins over score-only baselines are modest on low-rank,
dense matrices and decisive where evaluation is sparse, noisy, unpaired, small-cohort,
or high-rank---the motivating regime.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}
All methods are released as a documented Python package (the \PKPS estimator, matrix
completion, and IRT baselines), with the data-preparation, experiment, and figure scripts
for both the synthetic study and the HELM suite; every experiment is seeded, all real-data
numbers are means over $16$ seeds (synthetic: $50$), and hyperparameters are selected by the
leak-free cross-validation described in Section~\ref{sec:method} (leakage is checked by an
automated test that corrupts one model's held-out scores and verifies its predictions are
unchanged). Appendix~\ref{app:details} lists the suite composition, embedding models, and
all hyperparameter settings.

\paragraph{\PKPS.} RBF query kernel with bandwidth selected per run by leave-one-model-out
$k$-NN ($k{=}8$) on the observed scores over the grid
$\sigma\in\{0.03,0.1,0.3,1,3\}\times\mathrm{median}$ pairwise query distance, plus the
near-delta (DKPS) limit; linear response kernel on real data, RBF on synthetic. Response
blocks are capped at $48$ PCA components per dataset and the shared query space at $64$;
each (model, query) pair uses one sampled response ($r{=}1$). Models are embedded by
classical MDS ($d{=}12$ on real data, fixed a priori and not tuned; $d$ = latent dimension
on synthetic).

\paragraph{Prediction head.} All embedding methods share one head: logit-transformed
scores, per-task standardization to unit variance, a two-way (model $+$ task) additive
bias, and a distance-weighted $k$-NN ($k{=}5$) regression of the residual on the model
embedding. Held-out cells are predicted directly; observed cells leave-one-out.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Matrix completion follows BenchPress \citep{benchpress}: logit
space, per-task standardization, two-way bias, rank-$2$ ALS with ridge $\lambda{=}0.1$,
averaged over $5$ random initializations; on observed cells it is cross-fit so a cell's
prediction never sees its own sample. IRT is a 1PL (Rasch) model on binary tasks: item
difficulties by maximum likelihood (L-BFGS) on the largest fully-observed
(model $\times$ item) block, per-model ability by bounded scalar MLE, clipped to
$[-4,4]$. The sample baseline is the cell's own $m$-query mean.

\paragraph{Ensemble.} On observed cells, the sample mean is shrunk toward the \PKPS
estimate with per-cell precision weight $e^2_{\PKPS}/(e^2_{\PKPS}+\hat p(1-\hat p)/m)$ on
the sample, where $e^2_{\PKPS}$ is the embedding's depth-weighted disagreement with the
observed samples of that model; on missing cells, \PKPS and matrix completion are blended
with a weight fit by held-out cross-validation against observed scores. No quantity ever
uses the held-out full-eval scores.

\paragraph{Sweeps and reporting.}\begin{sloppypar} All real-data sweeps use $16$ seeds; curves and table
entries report the per-seed mean over tasks, then the mean ($\pm1$ SEM) over seeds. Query
efficiency: budgets $m\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}$ at full cohort and coverage; cohort
$n\in\{10,20,40,60,93\}$ (HELM) or $\{10,20,30,45\}$ (EEE) and task coverage
$p_{\mathrm{task}}\in\{0.2,0.35,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$, both at the few-query point $m{=}2$.
Completion: task coverage as above and query depth
$p_{\mathrm{query}}\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0\}$ with cohort lines $n\in\{10,\text{full}\}$;
task count $T\in\{4,8,12,18\}$ (HELM) or $\{4,8,12,16\}$ (EEE); the cohort sweep holds
$p_{\mathrm{task}}{=}p_{\mathrm{query}}{=}0.5$.\end{sloppypar}

\paragraph{Synthetic study.} $50$ seeds per configuration; latent dimension $5$, observed
embedding dimension $20$, score noise $0.1$, response noise $0.5$; both kernels RBF;
$k$-NN regression with $k{=}5$. Panel-specific settings are given in each figure title.

\paragraph{Compute.} All experiments run on a single multi-core CPU node; text embeddings
are precomputed once through the embedding API and cached. Embedding the EEE suite

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}}
\label{app:proof}

\paragraph{Population quantities.} Queries for model $a$ are drawn i.i.d.\ from a design
distribution over embedded queries; write $z=(u,x_a(u))$ for an embedded query--response
pair (response noise included) and $P_a$ for its law. By Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} the kernels are bounded:
$k_Q\in[0,1]$ after normalization (RBF) and $|k_R|\le B_R$. Define the population affinity
\[
A^*_{ab}\;=\;\frac{\mathbb{E}\,[k_Q(u,u')\,k_R(x_a(u),x_b(u'))]}{\mathbb{E}\,[k_Q(u,u')]},
\qquad z\sim P_a,\; z'\sim P_b \text{ independent},
\]
population squared distances $D^{*2}(a,b)=A^*_{aa}+A^*_{bb}-2A^*_{ab}$, the centered Gram
matrix $B^*=-\tfrac12 J D^{*2} J$ with $J=I-\tfrac1n\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$, and
population perspectives $\Psi^*\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ its rank-$d$ spectral embedding,
which exists with $\lambda_d(B^*)>0$ by Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} (cf.\ Definition~\ref{def:pop}).

\begin{lemma}[Affinity concentration]
\label{lem:conc}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:reg}, for models $a,b$ with $\min(|Q_a|,|Q_b|)\ge m$ and any
$\delta\in(0,1)$: with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\[
|A_{ab}-A^*_{ab}|\;\le\;\frac{2(1+B_R)}{\kappa^2}\,t_m(\delta),
\qquad t_m(\delta)=\bar B\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2m}},\quad \bar B=\max(2B_R,1),
\]
provided $t_m(\delta)\le\kappa/2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\begin{sloppypar}
The numerator $N=\frac{1}{|Q_a||Q_b|}\sum_{j,l}k_Q(u_j,u'_l)\,k_R(x_{aj},x_{bl})$ and
denominator $Z=\frac{1}{|Q_a||Q_b|}\sum_{j,l}k_Q(u_j,u'_l)$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:A} are
two-sample $U$-statistics of order $(1,1)$ in the i.i.d.\ pairs $z_j\sim P_a$,
$z'_l\sim P_b$, with kernels of range at most $2B_R$ and $1$ respectively (response noise
enters only through the bounded kernel argument, so the number of responses per query may
stay fixed). By Hoeffding's inequality for two-sample $U$-statistics
\citep[Section~5]{hoeffding1963}, each deviates from its mean by more than its range times
$\sqrt{\log(4/\delta)/2m}$ with probability at most $\delta/2$; since $\bar B$ dominates
both ranges, a union bound gives $|N-N^*|\le t_m(\delta)$ and $|Z-Z^*|\le t_m(\delta)$
jointly with probability $\ge1-\delta$. On this event, since $Z^*\ge\kappa$ by Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} and $t_m(\delta)\le\kappa/2$,
we have $Z\ge\kappa/2$, and
\[
|A_{ab}-A^*_{ab}|=\frac{|NZ^*-N^*Z|}{ZZ^*}
\;\le\;\frac{|N-N^*|}{Z}+\frac{|N^*|\,|Z-Z^*|}{ZZ^*}
\;\le\;\frac{2\,t_m(\delta)}{\kappa}+\frac{2B_R\,t_m(\delta)}{\kappa^2}
\;\le\;\frac{2(1+B_R)}{\kappa^2}\,t_m(\delta),
\]
using $|N^*|\le B_R$ and $\kappa\le1$.
\end{sloppypar}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Perspective perturbation]
\label{lem:pert}
Let $\varepsilon_\infty=\max_{a,b}|A_{ab}-A^*_{ab}|$. There is an orthogonal
$W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ such that
\[
\max_i\;\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\;\le\;
\frac{25\sqrt{d}\;n\,\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}\,.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Entrywise, $|D^2-D^{*2}|\le4\varepsilon_\infty$, and double centering inflates an
entrywise bound by at most a factor of $4$, so $E=B-B^*$ satisfies
$\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le\|E\|_F\le8\,n\,\varepsilon_\infty$. Let $B_d$ be the top-$d$
truncation of $B$ that classical MDS embeds ($B_d=\hat\Psi\hat\Psi^\top$, positive
semidefinite). By Weyl's inequality every eigenvalue of $B$ beyond the $d$-th, and every
negative eigenvalue, has magnitude at most $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ (since
$\lambda_{d+1}(B^*)=0$ and $B^*\succeq0$), so
$\|B_d-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$. The matrix $B_d-B^*$ has rank at most
$2d$, hence
\[
\|B_d-B^*\|_F\;\le\;\sqrt{2d}\,\|B_d-B^*\|_{\mathrm{op}}
\;\le\;\sqrt{2d}\,\big(\|B_d-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}\big)
\;\le\;2\sqrt{2d}\,\|E\|_F .
\]
Both $B_d=\hat\Psi\hat\Psi^\top$ and $B^*=\Psi^*\Psi^{*\top}$ are positive semidefinite of
rank at most $d$, so by the Procrustes alignment lemma of
\citet[Lemma~5.4]{tu2016procrustes},
\[
\min_{W\in O(d)}\;\|\hat\Psi-\Psi^*W\|_F^2
\;\le\;\frac{\|B_d-B^*\|_F^2}{2(\sqrt2-1)\,\lambda_d(B^*)}\,.
\]
Combining, $\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le\|\hat\Psi-\Psi^*W\|_F\le
\frac{2\sqrt{2d}}{\sqrt{2(\sqrt2-1)}}\cdot\frac{8n\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}
\le\frac{25\sqrt d\,n\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}$. Nearest-neighbor
distances are invariant to $W$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}.} Take scores in $[0,1]$ without loss of
generality (Assumption~\ref{ass:score}). Fix $\epsilon>0$ and set targets $c\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}/(8\gamma)$ for
the estimation error and $\epsilon'\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}/(2\gamma)$ for the population
nearest-neighbor distance. By Assumption~\ref{ass:support} there is $n_0(\epsilon')$ such that for $n\ge n_0$, a
reference model within population distance $\epsilon'$ of the target exists except with
probability at most $\epsilon/8$. Fix such an $n$. Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:conc} with
confidence $\delta=\epsilon/(8n^2)$ to every pair, a union bound and
Lemma~\ref{lem:pert} give $\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le c$ except with probability at
most $\epsilon/8$, once
\[
m\;\ge\;C'\,\frac{d\,(1+B_R)^2\,\bar B^2\,n^2\log(n/\epsilon)}{\kappa^4\,\lambda_d\,c^2}
\;=\;\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\kappa,1/\epsilon),
\]
for an absolute constant $C'$ (this choice also satisfies the proviso
$t_m\le\kappa/2$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:conc}), and does not involve the pairing fraction
$\rho$. On the intersection of the two
events, for a target model $f$ with nearest estimated reference neighbor $f^*$
(perspectives $\psi^*,\hat\psi^*$; the target's $\psi,\hat\psi$),
\[
|\hat y(f)-y(f)|\;\le\;\gamma\,\|\psi^*-\psi\|\;\le\;
\gamma\big(\|\hat\psi^*-W\psi^*\|+\|\hat\psi^*-\hat\psi\|+\|\hat\psi-W\psi\|\big)
\;\le\;\gamma\,(2c+\hat\delta),
\]
using Assumption~\ref{ass:score} and orthogonal invariance. The estimated nearest-neighbor distance
$\hat\delta$ is at most the estimated distance to the $\epsilon'$-close reference, itself
at most $\epsilon'+2c$ by the same alignment argument, so
$|\hat y-y|\le\gamma(4c+\epsilon')\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}$. Squaring, and adding the two
failure events (squared errors are bounded by $1$),
$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat y)\le\epsilon/2+\epsilon/4\le\epsilon$. \hfill$\qed$

\paragraph{Contrast with paired DKPS.} With $k_Q=\delta$, only identically-shared query
pairs enter the double sum, so the $U$-statistic runs over the $\rho M_{ij}$ shared queries
and the rate in Lemma~\ref{lem:conc} becomes $O_P\big((\rho M_{ij})^{-1/2}\big)$: DKPS's
guarantee requires $\rho M_{ij}\to\infty$ and vacates in the unpaired limit $\rho\to0$,
where its distance is undefined (Section~\ref{sec:method}). This asymmetry motivates
Conjecture~\ref{conj:qeff}; a formal lower bound for DKPS is future work.
