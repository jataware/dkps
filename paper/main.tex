\begin{abstract}
Evaluating a generative model on all queries from all available benchmark tasks is
prohibitively expensive. As such, models are often evaluated on different subsets of tasks
and/or different subsets of queries from a given task. The resulting collection of
responses and the corresponding (model, task) score matrix are unpaired and sparse. In
this paper, we introduce the Product Kernel Perspective Space (\PKPS), a collection of
low-dimensional representations of models based on their embedded responses. \PKPS
generalizes existing methods by comparing responses to similar, rather than just
identical, queries, so models that have responded to different queries are comparable with minimal
information lost. Theoretically, we show that
benchmark prediction using \PKPS is query-efficient relative to methods that only use
information from queries all models have responded to. Empirically, we show that \PKPS
outperforms relevant baselines on two tasks---query-efficient evaluation and (model, task)
score-matrix completion---across two large benchmark suites.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Generative models are evaluated on an ever-growing collection of benchmarks spanning
knowledge, reasoning, code, translation, and safety \citep{helm}. Evaluating a model on
all of them is prohibitively expensive, so models are evaluated on different subsets of
tasks and of queries within a task: a recent compilation of $84$ frontier models on
$133$ benchmarks finds only $23\%$ of the score matrix filled \citep{benchpress}.
Outside curated leaderboards the observed queries also differ across models: arena-style
platforms score each model on the conversations its users generate
\citep{chiang2024chatbot}, contamination-aware benchmarks refresh their items
\citep{white2024livebench}, and cost-driven subsampling gives each evaluation run its
own item subset \citep{perlitz2024efficient}. The resulting (model $\times$ task)
score matrix is therefore sparse, its entries are noisy averages over few queries, and
the responses that produced it are \emph{unpaired}: comparing models in general
requires using responses to different queries. Predicting the matrix's missing and true
entries would let practitioners reuse cached evaluations instead of paying to re-run
them \citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks, vivek2024anchor, benchpress}; for deprecated models
that can no longer be queried, prediction from cached responses is the only option.

Two lines of work predict scores rather than compute them: efficient benchmarking
extrapolates a task's full score from a small scored subset of its items, classically by
item-response theory \citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks}, and matrix completion imputes
cells that were never run from low-rank structure \citep{benchpress}. These are two
ends of one problem---every cell is observed with some number of queries, possibly
zero---but both use only each cell's aggregate score, and the responses behind those
scores carry more information than the scores themselves. The Data Kernel Perspective
Space (DKPS) \citep{helm2024tracking} uses this information, embedding each model from
its responses by classical multidimensional scaling and regressing benchmark scores in
the resulting space \citep{dkps_qeff}. Its distances, however, are defined only over
responses to shared queries---a \emph{paired} design, which in practice restricts
analysis to maximal subsets of models sharing a common query set. To our knowledge, no
existing method predicts the score matrix from evaluation data as it is typically
available: sparse, noisy, and unpaired.

In this paper, we introduce the \textbf{Product Kernel Perspective Space (\PKPS)}, a
collection of low-dimensional representations of models based on their embedded
responses. \PKPS compares every response of one model to every response of another,
weighting each comparison by the similarity of the queries that produced it: the
comparison between two models is a product of a query kernel and a response kernel,
normalized by
the total query-kernel mass, so that two models are not deemed dissimilar merely because
they answered different sets of queries (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}). Models are embedded
from these comparisons by classical multidimensional scaling, and paired DKPS is recovered
exactly when the query kernel is the identity (Section~\ref{sec:method});
Table~\ref{tab:suite} previews our main results.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We make two primary contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \emph{Methodological \& theoretical:} we extend the DKPS and its
query-efficiency theory to the unpaired regime. Benchmark prediction in the \PKPS is
query-efficient relative to the sample score, with a query budget that does not depend
on the fraction of queries shared across models, and relative to paired DKPS at every
pairing fraction (Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} and Corollary~\ref{cor:sample}).
\item \emph{Empirical:} on two benchmark suites---$93$ models on $18$ HELM tasks and
$45$ modern models on $16$ tasks from the Every Eval Ever datastore
\citep{eee2026}---we demonstrate utility on two problems: query-efficient evaluation,
where \PKPS matches the accuracy of the sample score with up to an $8\times$ smaller
query budget, and score-matrix completion, where ensembling \PKPS with low-rank matrix
completion reduces error by up to $28\%$ (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Problem setting and notation}
\label{sec:setting}
Our goal is to predict the score of a model for a task for which its responses are
partially observed or unobserved in the context of sparse and unpaired previously
evaluated reference models. For our purposes, a \emph{model} is a random mapping
$f:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{X}$ from a query space to a response space. The response
$f(q)$ is an independent sample from $P_{q}$. We observe $n$ models $f_1,\dots,f_n$,
where each model responds to a (potentially different) collection of queries
$Q_i\subseteq\mathcal{Q}$. The setting where $Q_{1}=\dots=Q_{n}$ (i.e., a \emph{paired}
design) is a special case. We assume access to responses
$\{\{f_{i}(q):q\in Q_{i}\}\}_{i=1}^{n}$.

Each query is an element of at least one of $T$
\emph{tasks}---$Q^{(1)},\dots,Q^{(T)}$---with $Q_{i}\subseteq\cup_{t=1}^{T}Q^{(t)}$
and $M^{(t)}:=|Q^{(t)}|$. Let $Q_{i}^{(t)}$ be the collection of queries from task $t$
that model $i$ has responded to and let $m_{i}^{(t)}:=|Q^{(t)}_{i}|$. The sparsity and
unpairedness of a task are functions of the $\{m_{i}^{(t)}\}$: it is \emph{sparse} when
few models have $m_{i}^{(t)}>0$, and \emph{unpaired} when the cross-model overlaps
$m_{ii'}^{(t)}:=|Q_{i}^{(t)}\cap Q_{i'}^{(t)}|$ are small. We let
$\rho_{ii'}^{(t)}:=m_{ii'}^{(t)}/m_{i}^{(t)}$ be the ratio of shared queries for a given
model pair and note that $\rho_{ii'}\approx1$ for all pairs in highly curated
leaderboards and $\rho_{ii'}\approx0$ in deployed evaluation settings.

Finally, let $y_i^{(t)}:=y(f_{i},Q^{(t)})$ be model $i$'s score on task $t$. For
$m_i^{(t)}>0$ we observe $y(f_{i},Q_i^{(t)})$. The goal of \emph{benchmark prediction}
is to estimate $y_i^{(t)}$. We consider two related instances of the problem: i)
estimate $y_{i}^{(t)}$ with $m_{i}^{(t)}>0$ (\emph{query efficiency}) and ii) estimate
$y_{i}^{(t)}$ with $m_{i}^{(t)}=0$ (\emph{matrix completion}).

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Low-dimensional representations of models.} Some approaches construct
representations by comparing internal activations across models
\citep{duderstadt2023comparing, huh2024platonic, horwitz2025atlas} or by comparing model
weights directly \citep{chen2025weights}. Evaluation frameworks, however, typically permit
only black-box access: they observe responses, not internals. The Data Kernel Perspective
Space (DKPS), introduced by \citet{helm2024tracking}, addresses this setting by
mapping a collection of models into a
low-dimensional Euclidean space via classical multidimensional scaling of average embedded
responses \citep{torgerson1952mds}. Prior work established consistency and concentration
of the representations \citep{acharyya2024consistent, acharyya2025concentration},
supervised inference thereon \citep{helm2025inference}, and query efficiency
\citep{dkps_qeff}. These constructions compare responses to shared queries, a paired
design. \PKPS replaces the identity matching with a product kernel so that models
evaluated on different queries remain comparable.

\paragraph{Benchmark prediction.} A second line of work predicts unobserved (model, task)
scores so that not every evaluation must be run; efficient benchmarking and matrix
completion are its two ends. \citet{polo2024tinybenchmarks} use item-response theory to
construct small item subsets whose scores predict full performance \citep[see
also][]{rasch1960, metabench}; \citet{vivek2024anchor} cluster items by cross-model
agreement and score only the cluster centers; \citet{bean2025scales} select items using
cognitive-scale embeddings; \citet{li2024active} select items adaptively via reinforcement
learning; and \citet{perlitz2024efficient} analyze the cost--reliability tradeoff of
benchmark designs. Closest to us, \citet{dkps_qeff} predict held-out scores from cached
responses.
On the completion end, \citet{benchpress} observe that the (model $\times$ task) score
matrix is approximately rank-two and complete it in logit space \citep[see
also][]{candes2009exact, mazumder2010softimpute, koren2009matrix}. These methods use only
each cell's aggregate score; \citet{benchpress} identify extending prediction to
instance-level outcomes as a natural next step. \PKPS unifies the two ends---a cell with
$m\ge0$ queries spans efficient benchmarking ($m{>}0$) and completion ($m{=}0$)---and
adds the response-level signal, which can be ensembled with matrix completion.

\section{The Product Kernel Perspective Space}
\label{sec:method}

Classical MDS consumes a distance matrix between the $n$ models of
Section~\ref{sec:setting}. When all models share a query set, the distance can be taken
directly between average embedded responses \citep{dkps_qeff}; when query sets differ,
there is nothing to subtract. Kernel comparisons \emph{across} two query sets remain
well-defined, however, so both paired DKPS and \PKPS first form an $n\times n$
comparison matrix $A$ (defined below) and induce the squared distance
$D^2(i,i')=A_{ii}+A_{i'i'}-2A_{ii'}$ (clamped at $0$)---the usual conversion of an inner
product to a squared distance, with $A_{ii'}$ in the role of an inner product between the
models' response behaviors. The $d$-dimensional
\emph{representations} are the classical-multidimensional-scaling minimizer
\begin{equation}
(\hat\psi_1,\dots,\hat\psi_n)=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{z_1,\dots,z_n\in\mathbb{R}^d}\
\sum_{i,i'=1}^{n}\big(\|z_i-z_{i'}\|-D(i,i')\big)^2,
\label{eq:mds}
\end{equation}
so model $i$ is the point $\hat\psi_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$, its \emph{perspective}. Paired DKPS
and \PKPS differ only in the comparison matrix $A$.

With all $n$
models answering the same $m$ queries
($Q_1=\dots=Q_n$), DKPS uses the identity-matched comparison
$A_{ii'}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_j k_R(x_{ij},x_{i'j})$: response $j$ of model $i$ is paired only
with response $j$ of model $i'$, so the design must be paired. With a linear $k_R$ the
induced distance is exactly the mean squared distance between aligned responses,
$D^2(i,i')=\tfrac1m\sum_j\|x_{ij}-x_{i'j}\|^2$.

\PKPS replaces
identity matching with a query kernel $k_Q$:
\begin{equation}
A_{ii'} = \frac{\sum_{j\in Q_i}\sum_{l\in Q_{i'}} k_Q(q_j,q_l)\, k_R(x_{ij},x_{i'l})}
              {\sum_{j\in Q_i}\sum_{l\in Q_{i'}} k_Q(q_j,q_l)},
\label{eq:A}
\end{equation}
Each term is normalized by its own query-kernel
mass, so models that answered different numbers of queries, or entirely different
queries, remain comparable; responses to similar queries contribute even across tasks, so
unpaired evaluations are used (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}). Paired DKPS is the special case
$k_Q=\delta$, where only $j=l$ survives.

We analyze and evaluate \PKPS with an RBF query kernel over the embedded queries,
$k_Q(q_j,q_l)=\exp(-\|u_j-u_l\|^2/2\sigma^2)$, with bandwidth $\sigma$ chosen per run
(below), and a linear response kernel $k_R(x,x')=\langle x,x'\rangle$; paired DKPS
corresponds to a linear $k_R$ with the identity query kernel $k_Q=\delta$. Linearity of
$k_R$ makes the kernel factorize across response domains
(Section~\ref{sec:setup}); the synthetic study uses an RBF $k_R$. Since Eq.~\ref{eq:A}
normalizes each pair by its own kernel mass, $A$ is only approximately an inner
product and need not be positive semidefinite---hence the clamp on $D^2$, and classical
MDS embeds the doubly-centered matrix by its top-$d$ nonnegative eigenvalues, as is
standard. $A_{ii'}$ costs $|Q_i||Q_{i'}|$ kernel terms per pair, and one response-kernel
pass serves the whole bandwidth grid.

\subsection{Theoretical properties of the \PKPS}
\PKPS is a strict generalization of DKPS. With $k_Q=\delta$ only equal queries lying in
both query sets survive the double sum of Eq.~\ref{eq:A}, so it reduces to paired DKPS
over the shared queries,
$A_{ii'}=\tfrac{1}{|Q_i\cap Q_{i'}|}\sum_{q\in Q_i\cap Q_{i'}}k_R(x_{iq},x_{i'q})$; since the RBF
kernel converges pointwise to $\delta$ as $\sigma\!\to\!0$, DKPS is also the
small-bandwidth limit. Conversely, for any strictly positive $k_Q$ the normalizer is
positive for any nonempty query sets, so $A_{ii'}$ is defined even on disjoint sets, where
paired DKPS is undefined. Between these limits, the bandwidth $\sigma$ controls the
tradeoff between exact matching and bridging.

\subsection{Inference in the \PKPS}
Inference is a two-step statistical problem, exactly as in the DKPS \citep{dkps_qeff}:
embed each model into the perspective space (Eq.~\ref{eq:mds}), then regress its
benchmark score on the resulting coordinate. On reference models with known
scores $\{(f_i,y_i)\}$ we fit $\hat h:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathcal{Y}$ on the pairs
$\{(\hat\psi_i,y_i)\}$ and predict $\hat y(f)=\hat h(\hat\psi(f))$. The free choices are
the bandwidth $\sigma$ and the regressor $\hat h$.

The optimal $\sigma$
depends on the degree of pairing in the data. We choose it per run by cross-validation
over a median-scaled grid $\sigma\in\{0.03,\dots,3\}\cdot\mathrm{med}$, selecting the
$\sigma$ whose perspective best predicts the \emph{observed} scores under
leave-one-model-out $k$-NN. Note that the selection criterion uses only observed scores
and never the held-out scores we report. Because the grid includes near-zero bandwidths,
cross-validation recovers paired DKPS when query overlap is high.

For each
replicate we (i) PCA-reduce response and query embeddings on the \emph{observed} rows
only (dimension by the second Zhu--Ghodsi elbow); (ii) form $D$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:A};
(iii) embed models by classical MDS; (iv) regress scores on the embedding, fit
leave-one-family-out. The score head---logit transform, per-task standardization, two-way
(model $+$ task) bias---is identical to the low-rank matrix-completion (LRMC)
baseline's. The two methods differ only in how the residual is modeled ($k$-NN on the
perspective versus a low-rank factorization), so performance differences are attributable
to the representation. We found matching the per-task standardization to be important:
without it, the model offset is dominated by the tasks with the largest logit variance.

The ensemble combines the \PKPS estimate with whichever score signal is available for
a cell. On an \emph{observed} cell, the score signal is the cell's own sample score, and
the blend weight is a scalar fit by the same leave-one-family-out cross-validation as the
regressor, against the reference models' cached full scores; the fitted weight grows with
the budget, so the sample score dominates as queries accumulate. On a \emph{missing}
cell, the score signal is LRMC \citep{benchpress}, blended with \PKPS by a weight
cross-validated on observed scores. No weight uses the scores of the family being
predicted. (A closed-form inverse-variance weighting performs slightly worse at every
budget; Appendix~\ref{app:abl}.)

\subsection{Query efficiency}
\label{sec:qeff}
DKPS requires query \emph{sets} to overlap; \PKPS requires only query
\emph{distributions} to overlap. This section makes the distinction precise, extending
the machinery of \citet{dkps_qeff}; MSE denotes mean squared error over the draw of a
target model from the model population.
\begin{definition}[Population \PKPS]
\label{def:pop}
Let each model $i$'s (query, response) pairs be drawn i.i.d.\ from a design distribution
$P_i$. The \emph{population comparison} is
$A^*_{ii'}=\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')\,k_R(x_i,x_{i'})]\,/\,\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')]$ with draws from
$P_i$ and $P_{i'}$ independent, the population squared distances are
$D^{*2}(i,i')=A^*_{ii}+A^*_{i'i'}-2A^*_{ii'}$, and the \emph{population perspectives}
$\psi_1,\dots,\psi_n\in\mathbb{R}^d$ are the rank-$d$ spectral embedding of the centered
Gram matrix $B^*$ of $D^{*2}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{assumption}[Score regularity]\label{ass:score}
The benchmark score is bounded and $\gamma$-Lipschitz on the population perspective space.
\end{assumption}
In practice, Assumption~\ref{ass:score} holds when models with similar response
distributions attain similar scores; this is the working premise of any response-based
predictor, and is visible as the smooth score gradient in Figure~\ref{fig:concept}(e).
\begin{assumption}[Model support]\label{ass:support}
The model distribution places mass on every neighborhood of the population perspective
space.
\end{assumption}
Assumption~\ref{ass:support} ensures that a target model has arbitrarily near reference
neighbors once the cohort is large; in practice it suggests that a large, diverse
reference population may be needed. Both conditions mirror \citet{dkps_qeff}.
\begin{assumption}[Kernel and geometry regularity]\label{ass:reg}
Kernels and embedded responses are bounded; the \emph{cross-model query-kernel mass} is
bounded below, $\kappa=\inf_{i,i'}\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')]>0$; and $B^*$ has rank $d$ with
spectral gap $\lambda_d>0$.
\end{assumption}
Boundedness and the spectral gap are standard conditions in spectral-embedding
analyses. The important condition for the unpaired setting is $\kappa>0$, the population
analogue of the pairing fraction $\rho$: for an RBF $k_Q$ it holds whenever the query
\emph{distributions} overlap, even when no individual query is shared. It fails when the
query distributions are disjoint, i.e., when models are evaluated on domains that the
kernel cannot bridge; in this case no unpaired method should be expected to compare
them.
\begin{proposition}[Concentration of \PKPS perspectives]
\label{prop:conc}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:reg}, with at least $m$ queries per model, for any
$\delta\in(0,1)$, with probability at least $1-\delta$ there is an orthogonal $W$ such
that
$\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le
C\kappa^{-2}\lambda_d^{-1/2}\sqrt{d}\,n\sqrt{\log(4n^2/\delta)/2m}$,
where $C$ depends only on the kernel bounds (proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}).
\end{proposition}
\begin{definition}[Query efficiency; following \citealp{dkps_qeff}]
\label{def:qeff}
Fix a per-model budget $m$ and let $h_n$ denote a score estimator using $n$ scored
reference models. $h$ is \emph{query-efficient relative to} $h'$ \emph{at budget} $m$ if
there exists $N$ such that $\mathrm{MSE}(h_n)\le\mathrm{MSE}(h'_n)$ with high
probability for all $n\ge N$, and \emph{query-efficient relative to} $h'$ if this holds
at every budget at which $\mathrm{MSE}(h'_n)$ is bounded away from zero.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}[Query efficiency of \PKPS]
\label{thm:qeff}
Fix a budget $m$ and pairing fraction $\rho\in[0,1]$, and let \PKPS-CV select its
bandwidth from a finite grid $\Sigma$ containing the exact-matching limit $k_Q=\delta$
by validation on held-out reference scores. Under
Assumptions~\ref{ass:score}--\ref{ass:reg}, for any $\epsilon>0$:
\emph{(i)} there exist $(n,m)$, with $m$ polynomial in $(1/\epsilon,1/\kappa,n)$ and
independent of $\rho$, at which $\mathrm{MSE}(\PKPS\text{-CV})\le\epsilon$ with high
probability;
\emph{(ii)} under a fully unpaired design (nonatomic design distributions) with
non-degenerate scores, $v=\mathrm{Var}(y)>0$, every paired-DKPS estimator has
$\mathrm{MSE}\ge v$ at \emph{every} $(n,m)$, so \PKPS-CV is query-efficient relative
to paired DKPS (Definition~\ref{def:qeff}) with margin at least $v-2\epsilon$;
\emph{(iii)} at every pairing fraction,
$\mathrm{MSE}(\PKPS\text{-CV})\le\mathrm{MSE}(\mathrm{DKPS})+\epsilon$ with high
probability for sufficiently many reference models, strictly whenever some bandwidth
improves on exact matching.
\end{theorem}
\begin{corollary}[Relative to the sample score]
\label{cor:sample}
The sample score's MSE at budget $m$ is $\sigma_q^2/m$ for every $n$, where
$\sigma_q^2$ is the per-query score variance, so by Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}(i), \PKPS-CV
is query-efficient relative to the sample score
whenever $\epsilon<\sigma_q^2/m$, at every pairing fraction.
\end{corollary}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
For (i), Proposition~\ref{prop:conc} bounds every perspective's estimation error; the
nearest-neighbor error splits into two estimation terms and the population
nearest-neighbor distance, which Assumption~\ref{ass:support} makes small for $n$
large; choosing $m=\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\kappa,1/\epsilon)$ gives
$\mathrm{MSE}\le\epsilon$, and no step involves $\rho$---exact matching, by contrast,
retains only the $\rho m$ shared pairs, so the same argument degrades as
$\rho\to0$. For (ii), with no shared queries every identity-matched comparison is an
empty sum, so a paired-DKPS prediction is a function of the reference data alone and its
error is at least the score variance; (i) supplies a bandwidth attaining
$\epsilon<v$. (iii) is model selection: the $\delta$ member of the grid coincides with
paired DKPS on the shared queries with the same regression head, and validation MSEs
concentrate uniformly over the finite grid (Hoeffding), giving an oracle inequality.
Full proofs in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}.
\end{proof}
Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} states that a query budget independent of the pairing fraction
suffices for any target accuracy (i); that under fully unpaired designs the separation
from paired DKPS is information-theoretic---with no shared queries, a paired-DKPS
prediction cannot depend on the target's responses at all (ii); and that
cross-validation extends the comparison safely to every pairing fraction (iii). Only
(iii) is a model-selection guarantee: (i) rests on the concentration of the
self-normalized product-kernel comparison, in which the population overlap $\kappa$
replaces the pairing fraction $\rho$ (Proposition~\ref{prop:conc}), and (ii) is an
impossibility result for exact matching. Empirically, a bridging bandwidth improves on
exact matching at every $\rho<1$ (Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic}e).

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
We evaluate \PKPS on synthetic data, where every lever of the observation process can be
swept, and on two heterogeneous benchmark suites. Throughout, the two instances of
Section~\ref{sec:setting} structure the experiments: query efficiency ($m_i^{(t)}>0$)
and matrix completion ($m_i^{(t)}=0$).

\subsection{Evaluation setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Synthetic data.} We generate $n$ models with latent ability vectors $v_i$ and
$T$ tasks with latent vectors $t_k$, scores $v_i\cdot t_k+\epsilon$, and queries drawn
near each task center. Each model answers $M_{ij}$ queries per task, of which $m_{ii'}$
are shared with another model (overlap $\rho=m_{ii'}/M_{ij}$); a task is observed with
probability $p_{\mathrm{task}}$ and a query with probability $p_{\mathrm{query}}$.

\paragraph{Benchmark suites.} The first suite is $18$ tasks across four HELM datasets
with very different response types---MATH ($7$ math subjects), WMT-14 ($5$ translation
directions), MedQA (medical multiple-choice), and LegalBench ($5$ legal classification
subsets)---on the $93$ models evaluated on all four. MATH and WMT have rich free-text
responses; MedQA and LegalBench responses are single tokens for which text embeddings are
degenerate, so we encode each dataset's responses natively (Gemini embeddings vs.\ one-hot
answers) in disjoint blocks. With a linear $k_R$ this makes cross-dataset response
similarity exactly zero, and a single shared query embedding with a within-domain
bandwidth keeps $k_Q$ near zero across domains, so the product kernel factorizes by
domain. The second suite, from the Every Eval Ever
datastore \citep{eee2026}, is $45$ modern models on $16$ tasks (MATH-MC levels $1$--$5$,
GSM-MC, GPQA-Diamond, and nine judging categories from JudgeBench and RewardBench~2), with
per-cell query pools drawn independently per model ($\sim\!10\%$ incidental
overlap)---unpaired by construction (Appendix~\ref{app:eee}).

\paragraph{Prediction methods.} We compare six predictors. The \emph{sample score} is a
cell's own $m$-query average. \emph{IRT} fits a one-parameter logistic model on the
largest shared item block \citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks}. \emph{Low-rank matrix
completion} (LRMC) is the logit-space imputation of \citet{benchpress} (missing cells only). \emph{DKPS}
and \emph{\PKPS} regress scores on the model embeddings of Section~\ref{sec:method},
DKPS being the identity-query-kernel limit. The \emph{ensemble} combines \PKPS with
whichever score signal a cell has (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\paragraph{Measurement.} Every experiment predicts held-out or true cell scores and
reports MAE against the full-evaluation score, mean $\pm1$ SEM over $16$ seeds ($50$ on
synthetic data). Sweeps vary one observation lever at a time, holding the others fixed
(grids in Appendix~\ref{app:details}); all hyperparameters, including the query
bandwidth, are chosen per run by the leak-free cross-validation of
Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{\PKPS outperforms DKPS on every observation lever.}
Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic} shows score MAE for \PKPS, DKPS, and the sample score as
each lever of the observation process is varied. \PKPS outperforms DKPS at every
setting, and both outperform the sample score. The margin is wide even when the data
is mostly paired (a--c), since bridging similar queries, across tasks as well as within,
supplies signal that exact matching does not use. The gap is largest when pairing is
scarce: as $p_{\mathrm{query}}$ drops (d), DKPS degrades while \PKPS is stable, and when
$\rho$ is swept directly (e), DKPS approaches the sample score as $\rho\!\to\!0$
while \PKPS is unaffected.

Panel (f) considers the \emph{denoising} form of the same problem: each cell is now
\emph{observed} with $M_{ij}$ noisy queries and we predict its true score. \PKPS with a
single query matches the accuracy that the sample score reaches at roughly five queries,
whether or not the queries are paired; DKPS denoises only when paired (dashed). At large
budgets the sample score becomes precise enough to overtake the embedding's residual
bias. This is the empirical counterpart of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}.

\paragraph{\PKPS-based methods outperform the sample score at small query budgets.}
Figure~\ref{fig:qe} shows MAE as a function of the query budget $m$. Each model answers
its own independently sampled queries (the real-data analogue of $\rho{=}0$; IRT is
poorly identified in this regime) and we predict the true full-evaluation score. At small
budgets the embedding-based methods are substantially more accurate than the sample
score: at $m{=}1$, \PKPS attains $0.136$ and the ensemble $0.125$ versus $0.292$, so
\PKPS with one query matches the sample score at four. As the budget grows, the sample
score eventually overtakes the embedding's residual bias ($0.034$ vs.\ $0.085$ at $m{=}32$);
the ensemble follows the better of its two components throughout. Panels (b, c) vary the
cohort and coverage: \PKPS improves steadily ($0.157\!\to\!0.124$,
$0.154\!\to\!0.128$; $35$--$37\%$ on the second suite) while DKPS, which cannot use
the additional unpaired responses, is flat.

\paragraph{The embedding completes missing cells where LRMC cannot.}
The same embedding also predicts cells that were \emph{never run}.
Figure~\ref{fig:completion} shows MAE on missing cells when a fraction
$p_{\mathrm{task}}$ of cells is observed (each at depth $p_{\mathrm{query}}$), against
the LRMC baseline (the sample score is unavailable for missing cells). LRMC requires many rows to estimate its factors and degrades when models are
scarce: at $n{=}10$ its MAE is $0.139$ versus $0.120$ for \PKPS
(Figure~\ref{fig:completion}, dashed). At the full cohort ($n{=}93$, solid) \PKPS
slightly edges LRMC ($0.103$ vs.\ $0.105$) and the ensemble performs best ($0.099$).

\paragraph{The per-cell gains are broad but tail-driven.} The Ensemble outperforms the
sample score on most cells, with the largest gains at the smallest budgets and on the
rich-response datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:qe}d--g); the win over LRMC is modest but
stays positive in the mean across datasets, cohorts, coverage, and query depth
(Figure~\ref{fig:completion}e--h).

\paragraph{Both applications replicate on the Every Eval Ever suite.} The second suite
shows the same qualitative behavior with a larger completion margin
(Table~\ref{tab:suite}; lever sweeps in Appendix~\ref{app:eee}): at $m{=}1$ \PKPS
attains a third of the sample score's error ($0.076$ vs.\ $0.224$), matching the
accuracy the sample score reaches at $m{=}8$ ($0.081$)---an $8\times$ smaller
budget---and on missing cells the
ensemble outperforms LRMC at \emph{every} operating point, including the full cohort
($0.069$ vs.\ $0.096$ at $n{=}45$). Table~\ref{tab:suite} collects both
applications on both suites; the ensemble is best or tied in every column.

\paragraph{The results are insensitive to representation and pipeline choices.} Varying
the MDS dimension over $4$--$24$ moves \PKPS's MAE by less than $0.005$, and the
cross-validated bandwidth matches the best fixed choice (Appendix~\ref{app:sens}).
Ablations of the regression head, the response kernel, the ensemble weighting rule, and
the incidental query overlap in the EEE pools are in Appendix~\ref{app:abl}.

\section{Discussion}
In this paper we introduced the \PKPS, which compares models through their responses
to similar, rather than identical, queries. Outside of curated leaderboards, evaluation
data is typically unpaired: each model answers the queries its evaluators chose, and
methods that require a shared block of identical queries do not apply. Our central finding is that benchmark prediction
remains possible under unpaired evaluation: as $\rho\!\to\!0$, DKPS and IRT degrade
sharply while \PKPS does not. The same embedding supports both applications we studied,
denoising cheap evaluations and completing never-run cells, and the ensemble performs at
least as well as either channel alone.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.} We close with the current limitations and the
extensions they suggest. First, \PKPS embeds every observed response and query with a
single fixed embedder; we did not ablate this choice, and embedding functions are known
to have a nonnegligible effect at small budgets \citep{dkps_qeff}. Second, the
block-diagonal instantiation on the HELM suite disables bridging across response domains;
a shared response space that would let responses in one domain inform another remains
untested. Third, computing $A$ is quadratic in the number of queries per model pair. Fourth,
Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}(i) is existential (it provides no rate in $n$), and the strict
margin of (iii) at intermediate $\rho$ rests on a bandwidth improving on exact matching,
which we establish empirically rather than by a lower bound. Finally, per-cell gains over
score-only baselines are modest on dense low-rank matrices; they are decisive in the
sparse, noisy, unpaired regime that motivates the problem.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}
All methods are released as a documented Python package (the \PKPS estimator, matrix
completion, and IRT baselines), with the data-preparation, experiment, and figure scripts
for both the synthetic study and the HELM suite; every experiment is seeded, all real-data
numbers are means over $16$ seeds (synthetic: $50$), and hyperparameters are selected by the
leak-free cross-validation described in Section~\ref{sec:method} (leakage is checked by an
automated test that corrupts one model's held-out scores and verifies its predictions are
unchanged). Appendix~\ref{app:details} lists the suite composition, embedding models, and
all hyperparameter settings.

\paragraph{\PKPS.} RBF query kernel with bandwidth selected per run by leave-one-model-out
$k$-NN ($k{=}8$) on the observed scores over the grid
$\sigma\in\{0.03,0.1,0.3,1,3\}\times\mathrm{median}$ pairwise query distance, plus the
near-delta (DKPS) limit; linear response kernel on real data, RBF on synthetic. Response
blocks are capped at $48$ PCA components per dataset and the shared query space at $64$;
each (model, query) pair uses one sampled response ($r{=}1$). Models are embedded by
classical MDS with $d{=}8$ throughout, following \citet{dkps_qeff} and fixed a priori
($d$ = latent dimension on synthetic). Appendix~\ref{app:sens} shows the results are
insensitive to $d$ over $4$--$24$, and that the cross-validated bandwidth tracks the best
fixed choice.

\paragraph{Prediction head.} All embedding methods share one head: logit-transformed
scores, per-task standardization to unit variance, a two-way (model $+$ task) additive
bias, and a distance-weighted $k$-NN ($k{=}5$) regression of the residual on the model
embedding, fit \emph{leave-one-family-out}: all models from the target's developer are
excluded from its training set, so predictions never lean on near-duplicate siblings
\citep{dkps_qeff}. Held-out cells are predicted directly; observed cells leave their own
target out.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Low-rank matrix completion (LRMC) follows BenchPress \citep{benchpress}: logit
space, per-task standardization, two-way bias, rank-$2$ ALS with ridge $\lambda{=}0.1$,
averaged over $5$ random initializations; on observed cells it is cross-fit so a cell's
prediction never sees its own sample. IRT is a 1PL (Rasch) model on binary tasks: item
difficulties by maximum likelihood (L-BFGS) on the largest fully-observed
(model $\times$ item) block, per-model ability by bounded scalar MLE, clipped to
$[-4,4]$. The sample baseline is the cell's own $m$-query mean.

\paragraph{Ensemble.} On observed cells, the prediction is $\alpha\,\hat
y_{\mathrm{sample}}+(1-\alpha)\,\hat y_{\PKPS}$ with a scalar $\alpha$ per held-out
family, selected on a $21$-point grid by leave-one-family-out validation against the
other families' cached full scores (the same information the regressor trains on); on
missing cells, \PKPS and LRMC are blended with a weight fit by held-out cross-validation
against observed scores. No quantity ever uses the scores of the family being predicted.
Appendix~\ref{app:abl} compares this rule to a closed-form inverse-variance weighting
and to the fixed $m/M$ schedule of \citet{dkps_qeff}.

\paragraph{Sweeps and reporting.}\begin{sloppypar} All real-data sweeps use $16$ seeds; curves and table
entries report the per-seed mean over tasks, then the mean ($\pm1$ SEM) over seeds. Query
efficiency: budgets $m\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}$ at full cohort and coverage; cohort
$n\in\{10,20,40,60,93\}$ (HELM) or $\{10,20,30,45\}$ (EEE) and task coverage
$p_{\mathrm{task}}\in\{0.2,0.35,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$, both at the few-query point $m{=}2$.
Completion: task coverage as above and query depth
$p_{\mathrm{query}}\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0\}$ with cohort lines $n\in\{10,\text{full}\}$;
task count $T\in\{4,8,12,18\}$ (HELM) or $\{4,8,12,16\}$ (EEE); the cohort sweep holds
$p_{\mathrm{task}}{=}p_{\mathrm{query}}{=}0.5$.\end{sloppypar}

\paragraph{Synthetic study.} $50$ seeds per configuration; latent dimension $5$, observed
embedding dimension $20$, score noise $0.1$, response noise $0.5$; both kernels RBF;
$k$-NN regression with $k{=}5$. Panel-specific settings are given in each figure title.

\paragraph{Compute.} All experiments run on a single multi-core CPU node; text embeddings
are precomputed once through the embedding API and cached. Embedding the EEE suite

\section{Pipeline ablations}
\label{app:abl}

Each ablation repeats the query-efficiency budget sweep ($16$ seeds, both suites)
with one pipeline choice changed; all other settings match Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\paragraph{Ensemble weighting.} We compare the paper's cross-validated scalar weight
against two alternatives: a closed-form inverse-variance rule (the sample score weighted
by $e^2_{\PKPS}/(e^2_{\PKPS}+\tilde p(1-\tilde p)/m)$ per cell, with $\tilde p$ the
\PKPS estimate and $e^2_{\PKPS}$ the depth-weighted, noise-debiased disagreement with
the observed samples) and the fixed schedule $\alpha=m/M$ of \citet{dkps_qeff}. The
cross-validated weight is best at every budget on both suites: on HELM it improves on the
inverse-variance rule from $0.132$ to $0.125$ at $m{=}1$ and from $0.077$ to $0.073$ at
$m{=}8$; on EEE from $0.084$ to $0.073$ and from $0.052$ to $0.049$. The $m/M$ schedule
trails both on HELM ($0.096$ at $m{=}8$) and is close to the cross-validated weight on
EEE, where the small pools ($M{=}32$) let $m/M$ reach $1$.

\paragraph{Regression head.} Replacing the distance-weighted $k$-NN head with ordinary
least squares helps slightly on HELM ($0.132$ vs.\ $0.136$ at $m{=}1$; $0.080$ vs.\
$0.085$ at $m{=}32$), consistent with \citet{dkps_qeff}, but hurts on EEE, increasingly
so at large budgets ($0.066$ vs.\ $0.055$ at $m{=}32$). We use $k$-NN as the robust
default across suites.

\paragraph{Response kernel.} An RBF response kernel in place of the linear one changes
\PKPS's MAE by at most $0.003$ at every budget on both suites; the linear choice is
justified by the block-diagonal factorization (Section~\ref{sec:setup}) at no accuracy
cost.

\paragraph{Incidental overlap.} The EEE pools share ${\sim}10\%$ of items across
models by chance. Enforcing exactly disjoint query sets (true $\rho=0$) changes \PKPS
by at most $0.005$ at $m\le8$ ($0.080$ vs.\ $0.076$ at $m{=}1$), and IRT becomes
inapplicable outright---no shared item block exists. At $m\ge16$ the disjointness
constraint exhausts the $32$-item pools and mechanically shortens the sample score's
effective depth, so comparisons there reflect the constraint rather than the methods.

\section{Proofs of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} and Corollary~\ref{cor:sample}}
\label{app:proof}

\paragraph{Population quantities.} Queries for model $i$ are drawn i.i.d.\ from a design
distribution over embedded queries; write $z=(u,x_i(u))$ for an embedded query--response
pair (response noise included) and $P_i$ for its law. By Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} the kernels are bounded:
$k_Q\in[0,1]$ after normalization (RBF) and $|k_R|\le B_R$. Define the population comparison
\[
A^*_{ii'}\;=\;\frac{\mathbb{E}\,[k_Q(u,u')\,k_R(x_i(u),x_{i'}(u'))]}{\mathbb{E}\,[k_Q(u,u')]},
\qquad z\sim P_i,\; z'\sim P_{i'} \text{ independent},
\]
population squared distances $D^{*2}(i,i')=A^*_{ii}+A^*_{i'i'}-2A^*_{ii'}$, the centered Gram
matrix $B^*=-\tfrac12 J D^{*2} J$ with $J=I-\tfrac1n\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$, and
population perspectives $\Psi^*\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ its rank-$d$ spectral embedding,
which exists with $\lambda_d(B^*)>0$ by Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} (cf.\ Definition~\ref{def:pop}).

\begin{lemma}[Affinity concentration]
\label{lem:conc}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:reg}, for models $i,i'$ with $\min(|Q_i|,|Q_{i'}|)\ge m$ and any
$\delta\in(0,1)$: with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\[
|A_{ii'}-A^*_{ii'}|\;\le\;\frac{2(1+B_R)}{\kappa^2}\,t_m(\delta),
\qquad t_m(\delta)=\bar B\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2m}},\quad \bar B=\max(2B_R,1),
\]
provided $t_m(\delta)\le\kappa/2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\begin{sloppypar}
The numerator $N=\frac{1}{|Q_i||Q_{i'}|}\sum_{j,l}k_Q(u_j,u'_l)\,k_R(x_{ij},x_{i'l})$ and
denominator $Z=\frac{1}{|Q_i||Q_{i'}|}\sum_{j,l}k_Q(u_j,u'_l)$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:A} are
two-sample $U$-statistics of order $(1,1)$ in the i.i.d.\ pairs $z_j\sim P_i$,
$z'_l\sim P_{i'}$, with kernels of range at most $2B_R$ and $1$ respectively (response noise
enters only through the bounded kernel argument, so the number of responses per query may
stay fixed). By Hoeffding's inequality for two-sample $U$-statistics
\citep[Section~5]{hoeffding1963}, each deviates from its mean by more than its range times
$\sqrt{\log(4/\delta)/2m}$ with probability at most $\delta/2$; since $\bar B$ dominates
both ranges, a union bound gives $|N-N^*|\le t_m(\delta)$ and $|Z-Z^*|\le t_m(\delta)$
jointly with probability $\ge1-\delta$. On this event, since $Z^*\ge\kappa$ by Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} and $t_m(\delta)\le\kappa/2$,
we have $Z\ge\kappa/2$, and
\[
|A_{ii'}-A^*_{ii'}|=\frac{|NZ^*-N^*Z|}{ZZ^*}
\;\le\;\frac{|N-N^*|}{Z}+\frac{|N^*|\,|Z-Z^*|}{ZZ^*}
\;\le\;\frac{2\,t_m(\delta)}{\kappa}+\frac{2B_R\,t_m(\delta)}{\kappa^2}
\;\le\;\frac{2(1+B_R)}{\kappa^2}\,t_m(\delta),
\]
using $|N^*|\le B_R$ and $\kappa\le1$.
\end{sloppypar}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Perspective perturbation]
\label{lem:pert}
Let $\varepsilon_\infty=\max_{i,i'}|A_{ii'}-A^*_{ii'}|$. There is an orthogonal
$W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ such that
\[
\max_i\;\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\;\le\;
\frac{25\sqrt{d}\;n\,\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}\,.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Entrywise, $|D^2-D^{*2}|\le4\varepsilon_\infty$, and double centering inflates an
entrywise bound by at most a factor of $4$, so $E=B-B^*$ satisfies
$\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le\|E\|_F\le8\,n\,\varepsilon_\infty$. Let $B_d$ be the top-$d$
truncation of $B$ that classical MDS embeds ($B_d=\hat\Psi\hat\Psi^\top$, positive
semidefinite). By Weyl's inequality every eigenvalue of $B$ beyond the $d$-th, and every
negative eigenvalue, has magnitude at most $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ (since
$\lambda_{d+1}(B^*)=0$ and $B^*\succeq0$), so
$\|B_d-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$. The matrix $B_d-B^*$ has rank at most
$2d$, hence
\[
\|B_d-B^*\|_F\;\le\;\sqrt{2d}\,\|B_d-B^*\|_{\mathrm{op}}
\;\le\;\sqrt{2d}\,\big(\|B_d-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}\big)
\;\le\;2\sqrt{2d}\,\|E\|_F .
\]
Both $B_d=\hat\Psi\hat\Psi^\top$ and $B^*=\Psi^*\Psi^{*\top}$ are positive semidefinite of
rank at most $d$, so by the Procrustes alignment lemma of
\citet[Lemma~5.4]{tu2016procrustes},
\[
\min_{W\in O(d)}\;\|\hat\Psi-\Psi^*W\|_F^2
\;\le\;\frac{\|B_d-B^*\|_F^2}{2(\sqrt2-1)\,\lambda_d(B^*)}\,.
\]
Combining, $\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le\|\hat\Psi-\Psi^*W\|_F\le
\frac{2\sqrt{2d}}{\sqrt{2(\sqrt2-1)}}\cdot\frac{8n\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}
\le\frac{25\sqrt d\,n\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}$. Nearest-neighbor
distances are invariant to $W$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}(i).} Take scores in $[0,1]$ without loss of
generality (Assumption~\ref{ass:score}). Fix $\epsilon>0$ and set targets $c\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}/(8\gamma)$ for
the estimation error and $\epsilon'\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}/(2\gamma)$ for the population
nearest-neighbor distance. By Assumption~\ref{ass:support} there is $n_0(\epsilon')$ such that for $n\ge n_0$, a
reference model within population distance $\epsilon'$ of the target exists except with
probability at most $\epsilon/8$. Fix such an $n$. Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:conc} with
confidence $\delta=\epsilon/(8n^2)$ to every pair, a union bound and
Lemma~\ref{lem:pert} give $\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le c$ except with probability at
most $\epsilon/8$, once
\[
m\;\ge\;C'\,\frac{d\,(1+B_R)^2\,\bar B^2\,n^2\log(n/\epsilon)}{\kappa^4\,\lambda_d\,c^2}
\;=\;\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\kappa,1/\epsilon),
\]
for an absolute constant $C'$ (this choice also satisfies the proviso
$t_m\le\kappa/2$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:conc}), and does not involve the pairing fraction
$\rho$. On the intersection of the two
events, for a target model $f$ with nearest estimated reference neighbor $f^*$
(perspectives $\psi^*,\hat\psi^*$; the target's $\psi,\hat\psi$),
\[
|\hat y(f)-y(f)|\;\le\;\gamma\,\|\psi^*-\psi\|\;\le\;
\gamma\big(\|\hat\psi^*-W\psi^*\|+\|\hat\psi^*-\hat\psi\|+\|\hat\psi-W\psi\|\big)
\;\le\;\gamma\,(2c+\hat\delta),
\]
using Assumption~\ref{ass:score} and orthogonal invariance. The estimated nearest-neighbor distance
$\hat\delta$ is at most the estimated distance to the $\epsilon'$-close reference, itself
at most $\epsilon'+2c$ by the same alignment argument, so
$|\hat y-y|\le\gamma(4c+\epsilon')\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}$. Squaring, and adding the two
failure events (squared errors are bounded by $1$),
$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat y)\le\epsilon/2+\epsilon/4\le\epsilon$. \hfill$\qed$

\paragraph{Relative to the sample score.} The sample score at budget $m$ is the mean of
$m$ i.i.d.\ per-query scores and uses no reference models, so its MSE is $\sigma_q^2/m$
for every $n$, where $\sigma_q^2$ is the per-query score variance ($\le1/4$ for scores
in $[0,1]$). For any $\epsilon<\sigma_q^2/m$ at the budget of
Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}(i), the estimator satisfies
$\mathrm{MSE}\le\epsilon<\sigma_q^2/m$ for all sufficiently large $n$: query efficiency
relative to the sample score in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:qeff}, proving
Corollary~\ref{cor:sample}.

\paragraph{Contrast with paired DKPS.} With $k_Q=\delta$, only identically-shared query
pairs enter the double sum, so the $U$-statistic runs over the $\rho m$ shared queries
and the rate in Lemma~\ref{lem:conc} becomes $O_P\big((\rho m)^{-1/2}\big)$: DKPS's
guarantee requires $\rho m\to\infty$ and vacates in the unpaired limit $\rho\to0$,
where its distance is undefined (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\paragraph{Proofs of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}(ii) and (iii).} Split the reference models into a training
set $T$ and a validation set $V$ with $|V|=n_v$. For each $\sigma\in\Sigma$, build the
perspective space on $T$, embed any model outside $T$ out of sample by its
Eq.~\ref{eq:A} comparison row to $T$ (the standard out-of-sample MDS coordinate), and let
$\hat y_\sigma$ be nearest-neighbor regression on $T$'s (perspective, score) pairs. The
selected bandwidth is $\hat\sigma=\arg\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\hat L(\sigma)$ with
$\hat L(\sigma)=\tfrac1{n_v}\sum_{i\in V}(\hat y_\sigma(f_i)-y_i)^2$. Conditional on
$T$, the validation losses are i.i.d.\ across the draw of validation models and bounded
in $[0,1]$, so by Hoeffding's inequality and a union bound over $\Sigma$, with
probability at least $1-\delta'$,
$\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}|\hat L(\sigma)-L(\sigma)|\le
\tau=\sqrt{\log(2|\Sigma|/\delta')/2n_v}$, where $L(\sigma)$ is the MSE on a fresh
target. On this event,
\[
L(\hat\sigma)\;\le\;\hat L(\hat\sigma)+\tau\;\le\;\hat L(\delta)+\tau
\;\le\;L(\delta)+2\tau\;=\;\mathrm{MSE}(\mathrm{DKPS})+2\tau,
\]
since the $k_Q=\delta$ member of the family coincides with paired DKPS on the shared
queries with the same regression head (Section~3.1). Taking $n_v$ large enough that
$2\tau\le\epsilon$ gives claim (iii) at every $(m,\rho)$. If some
$\sigma^\star$ has $\Delta=L(\delta)-L(\sigma^\star)>0$, the same event gives
$L(\hat\sigma)\le L(\sigma^\star)+2\tau=\mathrm{MSE}(\mathrm{DKPS})-\Delta+2\tau$,
strict for $2\tau<\Delta$. Finally, at $\rho=0$ with nonatomic designs,
$\Pr(q_j=q'_l)=0$ for every pair of independently drawn queries, so almost surely every
identity-matched sum is empty: any measurable completion of the DKPS member predicts as a
function of the reference data alone, independent of the target, whence
$\mathrm{MSE}(\mathrm{DKPS})=v+\mathbb{E}[(\mathbb{E}y-\hat y)^2]\ge v$, while under
Assumptions~\ref{ass:score}--\ref{ass:reg} part (i) supplies a bandwidth
attaining $\mathrm{MSE}\le\epsilon$, so $\Delta\ge v-\epsilon$ and the margin is at
least $v-\epsilon-2\tau\ge v-2\epsilon$. In practice we use leave-one-model-out
validation and a joint embedding (Section~\ref{sec:method}); the split protocol is
analyzed for independence of the validation losses. \hfill$\qed$
