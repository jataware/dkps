\title{Predicting Benchmark Scores from Sparse and Unpaired Cached Responses}
\author{Anonymous}

\begin{abstract}
Evaluating a generative model on all queries from all available benchmark tasks is
prohibitively expensive. As such, models are often evaluated on different subsets of tasks
and/or different subsets of queries from a given task. The resulting collection of
responses and the corresponding (model, task) score matrix are unpaired and sparse. In
this paper, we introduce the Product Kernel Perspective Space (\PKPS), a collection of
low-dimensional representations of models based on their embedded responses. \PKPS
generalizes existing methods for representing black-box generative models by comparing responses to similar -- rather than just
identical -- queries and is therefore able to utilize all available information. Theoretically, we show that
benchmark prediction using \PKPS is query-efficient relative to methods that only use information from dense and paired portions of the evaluation data.
Empirically, we show that \PKPS
outperforms relevant baselines on two tasks across two large benchmark suites.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
Evaluating a generative model is necessary for understanding its capabilities.
As the capabilities of generative models increase \citep{wei2022emergent, bubeck2023sparks},
their applicability expands to ever more domains---often necessitating the creation of
new benchmarks for, e.g., mathematics \citep{hendrycks2021math}, law
\citep{guha2023legalbench}, finance \citep{islam2023financebench}, venture capital
\citep{chen2025vcbench}, agentic tasks \citep{liu2023agentbench}, and even running a
vending machine business \citep{backlund2025vendingbench}.
The current benchmarking landscape is correspondingly vast: BIG-bench alone contains
more than 200 tasks \citep{srivastava2023beyond}, HELM tracks dozens of scenarios
across multiple releases \citep{helm}, and a recent compilation includes 133 different
benchmarks for frontier models \citep{benchpress}.
As such, a model is rarely evaluated on all benchmarks: of the (model, task) pairs in
the compilation above, only 23\% have a reported score \citep{benchpress}.
Further, as benchmarks evolve -- queries are added \citep{chollet2025arcagi2}, refreshed to avoid contamination
\citep{white2024livebench}, or subsampled to reduce cost
\citep{perlitz2024efficient} -- two models evaluated on the same benchmark may have
responded to different sets of queries.

Benchmark prediction \citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks, vivek2024anchor, li2024active,
hofmann2025fluid, bowyer2026efficient, benchpress}
attempts to alleviate the resource burden of evaluation by predicting scores from
already-available information rather than computing them just from newly generated
responses.
There are two main benchmark prediction tasks: query-efficient evaluation and
(model, task) score completion.
In query-efficient evaluation, a model responds to a small subset of a task's queries
and its score on the full task is predicted from the resulting responses.
Existing methods for query-efficient evaluation require access to item-level scores \citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks, hofmann2025fluid, li2024active,
bowyer2026efficient} and/or cached embedded responses \citep{dkps_qeff} to queries that all models have responded to and are typically not effective in settings where different models have been evaluated on different sets of queries.
In (model, task) score completion, a model has not responded to any of a task's
queries and its score on the task is predicted from the observed entries of the score
matrix.
Existing methods for score completion use only the aggregate scores and discard the
item-level responses that produced them \citep{benchpress}.
In both tasks, the collection of available responses is often unpaired
and sparse.

In this paper, we introduce the Product Kernel Perspective Space (\PKPS) to predict
benchmark scores directly from sparse, unpaired collections of responses.
Our contributions can be summarized as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
[wide, labelwidth=1pt, labelindent=12pt]
\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \emph{Conceptual:} we unify the two main benchmark prediction
tasks as instances
of a single problem: predicting scores from a sparse, unpaired collection of
responses from previously evaluated models.
\item \emph{Methodological \& theoretical:} we propose the \PKPS, a collection of
low-dimensional representations of models defined on potentially unpaired embedded
responses, and show that benchmark prediction using \PKPS is at least as
query-efficient as methods that only use information from paired responses
(Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}).
\item \emph{Empirical:} we show
that \PKPS matches the accuracy of the sample score with up to an $8\times$ smaller
query budget and, when ensembled with low-rank matrix completion, reduces score
matrix completion error by up to 28\%. Our empirical evaluation spans 2 large benchmark suites.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Benchmark prediction.}
We decompose benchmark prediction into two lines of prior work: query-efficient
benchmarking and (model, task) score completion.
Methods for query-efficient benchmarking predict a model's score on a full task from
its responses to a small subset of the task's queries---typically by selecting an
informative query subset and extrapolating via IRT models
\citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks, hofmann2025fluid}, clustering
\citep{vivek2024anchor}, rank-preserving subsampling \citep{perlitz2024efficient},
learned acquisition policies \citep{li2024active}, regression on item-level scores
\citep{bowyer2026efficient}, or regression on model representations
\citep{dkps_qeff}.
All previous methods proposed for query-efficient benchmarking implicitly require
paired responses from previously evaluated models.

Methods for (model, task) score completion predict entries of the score matrix that
were never evaluated, via cross-task predictability \citep{ye2023predictable},
observational scaling laws \citep{ruan2024observational}, or low-rank matrix
completion \citep{benchpress}. All operate exclusively on aggregate scores and
discard information in the item-level scores or responses.
Our proposed method uses item-level information from sparse and unpaired evaluation
data to improve on methods from both literatures.

\paragraph{Low-dimensional representations of models.}
Our approach to sparse and unpaired benchmark prediction first constructs low-dimensional representations of the models before learning a simple regressor in the representation space.
Existing approaches to represent models vary by access regime.
For example, representations can be induced by comparing internal activations
\citep{duderstadt2023comparing, huh2024platonic} and/or weights
\citep{chen2025weights} in the white-box setting.
Evaluators do not necessarily have white-box access and instead rely on black-box methods such as
the Data Kernel Perspective Space (DKPS) \citep{helm2024tracking}, which constructs low-dimensional representations by applying multidimensional scaling to
distances between matrices of embedded responses on a paired set of queries.
Recent theoretical work has established desirable theoretical properties such as consistency, concentration, and applicability to inference \citep{acharyya2024consistent,
acharyya2025concentration, helm2025inference}.
Most relevant to our setting, \citet{dkps_qeff} regress benchmark scores of
previously evaluated models on DKPS representations and prove that the resulting
predictions are query-efficient relative to the sample score.
The proposed Product Kernel Perspective Space generalizes this construction with a kernel on the query space, enabling comparisons in the sparse and unpaired evaluation regime.

\section{Benchmark Prediction}
\label{sec:setting}
For our purposes, a model is a random mapping
$f:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{X}$ from a query space to a response space.
A task is a pair $(Q^{(t)}, s^{(t)})$, where $Q^{(t)}\subseteq\mathcal{Q}$ is a
collection of queries with $M^{(t)}:=|Q^{(t)}|$ and $s^{(t)}$ is a scoring function
mapping a model and a query collection to a score. Model $i$'s true score on task
$t$ is $y_i^{(t)}:=s^{(t)}(f_{i},Q^{(t)})$.

Evaluation data is indexed by (model, task, query) triples: for $n$ models
$f_1,\dots,f_n$ and $T$ tasks $Q^{(1)},\dots,Q^{(T)}$, the response of model $i$ to
query $q\in Q^{(t)}$ may or may not have been generated.
Let $Q_{i}^{(t)}\subseteq Q^{(t)}$ be the queries for which model $i$'s response is
observed, with $m_{i}^{(t)}:=|Q^{(t)}_{i}|$ and $Q_i:=\cup_{t}Q_i^{(t)}$.
The corresponding \emph{sample score}
$\tilde{y}_{i}^{(t)}:=s^{(t)}(f_{i},Q_{i}^{(t)})$ is a noisy estimate of
$y_{i}^{(t)}$.

Evaluation data is \emph{sparse} when few models have
$m_{i}^{(t)}>0$ for a given task, and \emph{unpaired} when the cross-model overlaps
$m_{ii'}^{(t)}:=|Q_{i}^{(t)}\cap Q_{i'}^{(t)}|$ are small. We let
$\rho_{ii'}^{(t)}:=m_{ii'}^{(t)}/m_{i}^{(t)}$ be the ratio of shared queries for a
given model pair and note that $\rho_{ii'}\approx1$ for all pairs in highly curated
leaderboards and $\rho_{ii'}\approx0$ in deployed evaluation settings.
The goal of benchmark prediction is to estimate a model's score from the available
evaluation data.
The two tasks introduced in \S\ref{sec:introduction} are the two regimes of the
observation count $m_i^{(t)}$:
\begin{enumerate}[wide, labelwidth=1pt, labelindent=12pt]
\setlength{\itemsep}{1pt}\setlength{\parskip}{0pt}
\item \emph{Query-efficient evaluation} is the case
with $m_{i}^{(t)}>0$. Responses or scores from other models on task $t$ are
typically available, and the goal is to outperform the naive sample score
$\tilde{y}_{i}^{(t)}$.
\item \emph{(Model, task) score matrix completion} is the case with $m_{i}^{(t)}=0$.
No responses from the target (model, task) slice exist and prediction
must be based on model $i$'s responses to other tasks and on responses from
other models evaluated on task $t$.
\end{enumerate}

\section{The Product Kernel Perspective Space}
\label{sec:method}

We estimate a model's score $y_i^{(t)}$ from the sparse, unpaired
evaluation data of Section~\ref{sec:setting} by constructing low-dimensional
representations of the models based on an appropriately defined pairwise distance
matrix.
We assume access to the responses for each observed (model, task, query) triple and
to two embedding functions $g_{\mathrm{query}}$ and $g_{\mathrm{response}}$ that map
queries and responses to $\mathbb{R}^{p_{Q}}$ and $\mathbb{R}^{p_{R}}$, respectively.
Let $q_{1},\hdots,q_{M}$ be an enumeration of all queries and let
$x_{ij}$ denote the embedded response $f_{i}(q_{j})$ and $u_{j}$ denote the embedded
query $q_{j}$.

Given a query kernel
$k_Q:\mathbb{R}^{p_{Q}}\times\mathbb{R}^{p_{Q}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}$ and a response
kernel $k_R:\mathbb{R}^{p_{R}}\times\mathbb{R}^{p_{R}}\to\mathbb{R}_{\ge0}$, we
compare models $i$ and $i'$ via
\begin{equation}
A_{ii'} = \frac{\sum_{j\in Q_i}\sum_{l\in Q_{i'}} k_Q(u_j,u_l)\, k_R(x_{ij},x_{i'l})}
              {\sum_{j\in Q_i}\sum_{l\in Q_{i'}} k_Q(u_j,u_l)}.
\label{eq:A}
\end{equation}
Each entry is normalized by its own query-kernel mass, so models that answered
different numbers of queries, or entirely different queries, remain comparable.
We use the induced squared distance $D^2(i,i')=A_{ii}+A_{i'i'}-2A_{ii'}$ to capture
model (dis)similarity \citep{gretton2012kernel}.
Finally, the Product Kernel Perspective Space representations of the
models---$\widehat{\psi}_{1},\hdots,\widehat{\psi}_{n}$---are defined as the
$d$-dimensional Euclidean configuration that minimizes the raw stress criterion to
$D$ \citep{trosset2024continuous}. That is,
\begin{equation}
(\hat\psi_1,\dots,\hat\psi_n)=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{z_1,\dots,z_n\in\mathbb{R}^d}\
\sum_{i,i'=1}^{n}\big(\|z_i-z_{i'}\|-D(i,i')\big)^2.
\label{eq:mds}
\end{equation}
We note that the definition of the \PKPS strictly generalizes previous work by
replacing the identity query kernel ($k_{Q}(u,u')=\indone\{u=u'\}$) with a generic
query kernel.
Importantly, for any strictly positive $k_Q$, such as an RBF over the query
embeddings, $D(i,i')$ includes information from unpaired responses and Eq.~\eqref{eq:A}
is defined even on disjoint query sets.
We highlight the difference between the previously proposed Data Kernel Perspective
Space and the \PKPS in Figure~\ref{fig:concept}.

\subsection{Theoretical properties}
\label{sec:theory}
We next establish theoretical properties of the \PKPS necessary for demonstrating
its efficiency in sparse and unpaired benchmark prediction.
In particular, the main result of this section shows that the representations
concentrate around their population counterparts at a rate governed by the overlap of
the models' designs.
We model the evaluation data of Section~\ref{sec:setting} as sampled (model, task,
response) triples.
Conditional on model $i$, each triple is an independent draw from its design
distribution $P_{i}$: a task is sampled first, then a query conditioned on the
sampled task, and finally the model's response to the query.
The population \PKPS is defined by replacing the sums of Eq.~\eqref{eq:A} with
expectations under the designs (Definition~\ref{def:pop},
Appendix~\ref{app:proof}).
Our analysis and implementation use the classical MDS solution: it coincides with the
minimizer of Eq.~\eqref{eq:mds} when $D$ is Euclidean and is its standard tractable
surrogate otherwise.

Our concentration results require bounded query and response kernels, bounded
embedded responses, a positive cross-model query kernel mass, and a rank-$d$
spectral gap in the population Gram matrix (see
Assumptions~\ref{ass:bound}--\ref{ass:spec}, Appendix~\ref{app:proof}).
The required boundedness and the spectral gap are standard in spectral-embedding
analyses \citep{yu2015useful, acharyya2025concentration}.
The atypical requirement in our setting is the positive cross-model query kernel
mass, though this holds trivially under a strictly positive $k_Q$ and importantly
does not assume that any individual query is shared.
Let $(\psi_1,\dots,\psi_n)$ denote the population \PKPS,
$\kappa=\inf_{i,i'}\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')]$ the cross-model query kernel mass, and
$\lambda_d$ the $d$-th largest eigenvalue of the population Gram matrix.
\begin{theorem}[Concentration of \PKPS perspectives]
\label{prop:conc}
Suppose Assumptions~\ref{ass:bound}--\ref{ass:spec} hold and that every model has
responded to at least $m$ queries, $\min_i|Q_i|\ge m$. Then for any
$\delta\in(0,1)$, with probability at least $1-\delta$ there is an orthogonal $W$ such
that
$\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le
C\kappa^{-2}\lambda_d^{-1/2}\sqrt{d}\,n\sqrt{\log(4n^2/\delta)/2m}$,
where $C$ depends only on the kernel bounds.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Each entry of Eq.~\eqref{eq:A} is a ratio of two-sample U-statistics.
We thus get concentration of numerator and denominator via Hoeffding's inequality
\citep{hoeffding1963} and the bounded kernel assumption.
The denominator is bounded below by $\kappa$ and gives entrywise error
$O(\kappa^{-2}m^{-1/2})$ (Lemma~\ref{lem:conc}).
A Weyl--Procrustes perturbation argument \citep[Lemma~5.4]{tu2016procrustes} then
transfers the entrywise error to the rank-$d$ spectral embedding at the stated rate
(Lemma~\ref{lem:pert}). The full proof is in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}.
\end{proof}
The rate of \PKPS concentration is the rate of paired DKPS concentration scaled by
the population overlap ($\kappa^{-2}$), and does not require paired queries.

\subsection{Inference in the \PKPS}
\label{sec:qeff}
We next describe benchmark prediction in the \PKPS and establish its query
efficiency.
Inference using the \PKPS is a two-step statistical problem: first embed the
models, then train a decision function that uses the representations as proxies
for the models \citep{dkps_qeff}.

Fix a task $t$ and let $f,f_{1},\hdots,f_{n}$ be i.i.d.\ draws from a model
distribution, observed through the sampled (model, task, response) triples of
Section~\ref{sec:theory}.
Every observed quantity is a function of the triples: the embedded responses enter
Eq.~\eqref{eq:A}, and the sample-score vector
$(\tilde{y}_{i}^{(1)},\hdots,\tilde{y}_{i}^{(T)})$ collects per-task statistics of
model $i$'s responses.
The true score $y_{i}:=s^{(t)}(f_{i},Q^{(t)})$ is a functional of the model and is
never observed.
The models with $m_{i}^{(t)}>0$ serve as \emph{references} for task $t$, and the
\emph{target} is the model whose true score we estimate---its own cell may be
observed but noisy (query-efficient evaluation) or missing (score completion).
We embed all $n{+}1$ models jointly via Eq.~\eqref{eq:mds}, treat $\hat\psi_{i}$
as a proxy for $f_{i}$, fit a regressor $\hat h:\mathbb{R}^{d}\to\mathcal{Y}$ on
the observed pairs $\{(\hat\psi_{i},\tilde{y}_{i}^{(t)})\}_{i=1}^{n}$, and
predict $\hat y(f)=\hat h(\hat\psi)$.
We note that the procedure is naturally transductive---Eq.~\eqref{eq:A} uses no
scores, so the target is embedded alongside the references---though out-of-sample
extensions exist \citep{trosset2008out}.

The main result of this section shows that benchmark prediction with the \PKPS is
query-efficient relative to paired DKPS.
Beyond Assumptions~\ref{ass:bound}--\ref{ass:spec}, our result requires a bounded
and Lipschitz score on the population perspective space---the working premise of
any response-based predictor (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}e)---and a model
distribution that places mass on every neighborhood of it, so that the target has
near reference neighbors (Assumptions~\ref{ass:score} and~\ref{ass:support},
Appendix~\ref{app:proof}).
In the following, MSE denotes mean squared error over the draw of the target
model and its triples,
$m$ denotes a per-model query budget, $\min_{i}|Q_{i}|\ge m$, and $\sigma_q^2$
denotes the per-query score variance.

\begin{definition}[Query efficiency; following \citealp{dkps_qeff}]
\label{def:qeff}
Fix a per-model query budget $m$ and let $h_n$ denote a score estimator using $n$
scored reference models.
$h$ is \emph{query-efficient relative to} $h'$ if there exists $N$ such that
$\mathrm{MSE}(h_n)\le\mathrm{MSE}(h'_n)$ with high probability for all $n\ge N$.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}[Query efficiency relative to paired DKPS]
\label{thm:qeff}
Fix a per-model budget $m$ and pairing fraction $\rho\in[0,1]$.
Let the scoring functions be means of bounded per-query scores, let each observed
cell be a uniform subsample at budget $m$, and write \PKPS$_\sigma$ for the
estimator with fixed bandwidth $\sigma$ and a $k$-nearest-neighbor regressor fit
on the sample scores $\tilde{y}_{i}^{(t)}$.
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:bound}--\ref{ass:support}, there exists a bandwidth
$\sigma$ such that \PKPS$_\sigma$ is query-efficient relative to paired DKPS.
Under a fully unpaired design ($\rho=0$), the separation is strict: every
paired-DKPS estimator has $\mathrm{MSE}\ge\mathrm{Var}(y)$ at every $(n,m)$, while
for any $\epsilon>0$ there exist $(n,m,k)$, with $m$ polynomial in
$(1/\epsilon,1/\kappa,n)$ and independent of $\rho$, at which
$\mathrm{MSE}(\PKPS_\sigma)\le\epsilon$ with high probability.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
The exact-matching limit coincides with paired DKPS on the shared queries with the
same regression head, which gives the first claim with equality.
With exact reference scores, Theorem~\ref{prop:conc} and
Assumption~\ref{ass:support} supply a bandwidth attaining any $\epsilon$ with
$m=\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\kappa,1/\epsilon)$, and no step involves $\rho$
(Lemma~\ref{lem:exact}).
The reference noises $\tilde{y}_{i}^{(t)}-y_{i}$ are mean zero, bounded, of
variance at most $\sigma_q^2/m$, and independent across models under unpaired
designs, so the $k$-neighbor average contributes at most $\sigma_q^2/(mk)$,
removed by letting $k$ grow.
With no shared queries, every identity-matched comparison is an empty sum, so a
paired-DKPS prediction cannot depend on the target's responses and its error is at
least the score variance.
The full proofs are in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}.
\end{proof}
\citet{dkps_qeff} established that paired DKPS is query-efficient relative to the
sample score.
The comparison extends to \PKPS at every pairing fraction.
\begin{corollary}[Relative to the sample score]
\label{cor:sample}
The sample score's MSE at budget $m$ is $\sigma_q^2/m$ for every $n$.
By the achievability part of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}, \PKPS is query-efficient
relative to the sample score whenever $\epsilon<\sigma_q^2/m$, at every pairing
fraction.
\end{corollary}
In practice, the bandwidth is selected by cross-validation over a finite grid
containing the exact-matching limit (Section~\ref{sec:experiments},
Appendix~\ref{app:proof}).
Empirically, a bandwidth that bridges similar queries improves on exact matching at
every $\rho<1$ (Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic}e).

The same machinery covers (model, task) score completion ($m_i^{(t)}=0$): since
Eq.~\eqref{eq:A} uses only responses, a model that has answered none of task $t$'s
queries is embedded from its responses to other tasks, and the regression transfers
the scores of reference models evaluated on $t$.
Identity-matched constructions have no mechanism for this regime and the sample
score does not exist.
We evaluate completion empirically in Section~\ref{sec:experiments}.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
We evaluate \PKPS on synthetic data and on two large benchmark suites.

\paragraph{Synthetic data.} We generate $n$ models with latent ability vectors
$v_i \in \mathbb{R}^{5}$ and $T$ tasks with latent vectors $t_k \in \mathbb{R}^{5}$,
scores $v_i\cdot t_k+\epsilon$, and queries drawn near each task center ($u_j = t_k +
\eta_j$ with isotropic noise $\eta_j$; a model's embedded response to $u_j$ is
centered at $u_j + v_i$).
Each model answers $M_{ij}$ queries per task. We control unpairedness via
$\rho=m_{ii'}/M_{ij}$ and sparsity via a probability of observing a task
$p_{\mathrm{task}}$ and, conditioned on observing a task, a probability of answering
a query $p_{\mathrm{query}}$.

\paragraph{Benchmark suites.}
We consider two large benchmark suites.
The first consists of $18$ tasks from HELM-Lite \citep{helm}. The tasks have very
different response types -- MATH (\citealp{hendrycks2021math}, $7$ math subjects),
WMT-14 (\citealp{bojar2014wmt}, $5$ translation directions), MedQA
(\citealp{jin2021medqa}, medical multiple-choice), and LegalBench
(\citealp{guha2023legalbench}, $5$ legal classification subsets).
We consider $93$ models that have been evaluated on all $18$ tasks.
As collected, the suite is dense and fully paired ($\rho_{ii'}=1$): we induce sparsity and unpairedness by subsampling each model's observed queries.

The second suite is built from the Every Eval Ever datastore \citep{eee2026}, a
community-maintained collection of item-level evaluation results covering $36$ benchmarks and $165$ models and growing.
The store exhibits the sparsity and unpairedness that motivate this paper:
only $15\%$ of (model, benchmark) pairs have been evaluated and $59\%$ of model
pairs share no benchmark at all.
Within a benchmark, pairedness is bimodal: on $26$ of $33$ multi-model benchmarks,
models answered essentially identical item sets (mean $\rho_{ii'}>0.99$), while the
remaining seven -- all arena-style \citep{chiang2024chatbot} -- are severely
unpaired (mean $\rho_{ii'}$ between $0.27$ and $0.66$).
We study a dense submatrix of the store so that true scores are available and
sparsity and pairedness can be controlled in our evaluations: $16$ tasks (MATH-MC
levels $1$--$5$ \citep{hendrycks2021math}, GSM-MC \citep{cobbe2021gsm8k},
GPQA-Diamond \citep{rein2023gpqa}, and nine judging categories from JudgeBench
\citep{tan2024judgebench} and RewardBench~2 \citep{malik2025rewardbench2}) and the
$45$ models that answered every item of every task.
We randomly sample $ 32 $ responses for each model for each task and treat the full benchmark score as the target.

Both suites were chosen because they include item-level responses -- most large
benchmark leaderboards only include overall or subtask scores.
Details describing the HELM and EEE data such as the included models, included
tasks, the number of queries per task, etc.\ are provided in
Appendices~\ref{app:details} and~\ref{app:eee}, respectively.

\paragraph{Methods and baselines.} We evaluate \PKPS on both query-efficiency and
(model, task) score prediction.
Unless otherwise stated we embed all responses and queries into a $3072$-dimensional
space via \texttt{gemini-embedding-001} \citep{lee2025gemini}, apply PCA on the
collection of embedded queries and embedded responses, and use an RBF kernel on the
reduced query space and a linear kernel on the reduced response space.
Given the \PKPS pairwise distance matrix, we embed the models in a $d = 8$ space and
use $k$-nearest neighbor regression with $k = 5$.

Depending on the task we include different baselines. Each produces an estimate of
$y_{i}^{(t)}$:
\begin{enumerate}[wide, labelwidth=1pt, labelindent=12pt]
    \item \emph{Query-efficiency}. We compare \PKPS to the sample score (i.e.,
    $\tilde{y}_{i}^{(t)}$), paired DKPS \citep{dkps_qeff}, Item Response Theory
    (``IRT'', \citealp{polo2024tinybenchmarks}) and a convex combination
    of \PKPS and sample score (i.e.,
    $\alpha\,\tilde{y}_{i}^{(t)}+(1-\alpha)\,\hat{y}^{(t)}_{i,\mathrm{PKPS}}$).
    \item \emph{Score completion.} We compare \PKPS to Low-Rank Matrix Completion
    (``LRMC'', \citealp{benchpress}) and a convex combination of \PKPS and LRMC
    (i.e., $\alpha\,\hat{y}^{(t)}_{i,\mathrm{LRMC}}+(1-\alpha)\,\hat{y}^{(t)}_{i,\mathrm{PKPS}}$).
\end{enumerate}
Both the query kernel RBF bandwidth and the convex coefficient for the ensemble are
selected via leave-one-out cross validation on the observed data.
We report average mean absolute error (MAE) of the predicted score versus the true
score for every setting over $16$ seeds ($50$ on synthetic data).
Additional experimental details, such as the sweep grids, PCA dimensions, and
cross-validation protocols, are provided in Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{\PKPS outperforms DKPS on every synthetic observation lever.}
Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic} shows score MAE for \PKPS, DKPS, and the sample score as
each lever of the observation process is varied.
\PKPS outperforms DKPS at every setting, and both outperform the sample score.
The margin is wide even when the data is mostly paired (a--c): bridging similar
queries supplies signal that exact matching does not use.
The gap is largest when pairing is scarce: as $p_{\mathrm{query}}$ drops (d), DKPS
degrades while \PKPS is stable, and when $\rho$ is swept directly (e), DKPS
approaches the sample score as $\rho\!\to\!0$ while \PKPS is unaffected.
In the denoising setting (f), where each cell is observed with $M_{ij}$ noisy
queries and we predict its true score, \PKPS with one query matches the accuracy the
sample score reaches at roughly five, whether or not the queries are paired.
This is the empirical counterpart of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}.

\paragraph{\PKPS-based methods outperform the sample score at small query budgets.}
Figure~\ref{fig:qe} shows MAE as a function of the query budget $m$.
Each model answers its own independently sampled queries, the real-data analogue of
$\rho{=}0$, and we predict the true full-evaluation score.
IRT is poorly identified in this regime.
At small budgets the representation-based methods are substantially more accurate than
the sample score: at $m{=}1$, \PKPS attains $0.136$ and the ensemble $0.125$ versus
$0.292$, so \PKPS with one query matches the sample score at four.
As the budget grows, the sample score eventually overtakes the representation's residual
bias ($0.034$ vs.\ $0.085$ at $m{=}32$).
The ensemble follows the better of its two components throughout.
Panels (b, c) vary the cohort and coverage: \PKPS improves steadily
($0.157\!\to\!0.124$ and $0.154\!\to\!0.128$ on HELM, $35$--$37\%$ relative on EEE)
while DKPS, which cannot use the additional unpaired responses, is flat.

\paragraph{The query-efficiency gains are broad but tail-driven.}
Figure~\ref{fig:qe}d--g shows the per-cell difference between the sample score's and
the ensemble's absolute errors, by dataset, budget, cohort, and coverage.
The ensemble outperforms the sample score on most cells and in the mean of every row.
The distribution of gains is skewed: the largest concentrate at the smallest budgets
and on the rich-response datasets, where a single response carries the most
information about the model.

\paragraph{The same representations complete missing cells.}
Figure~\ref{fig:completion}a--d shows MAE on missing cells when a fraction
$p_{\mathrm{task}}$ of cells is observed, each at depth $p_{\mathrm{query}}$.
The sample score does not exist for a missing cell, so the comparison is against LRMC.
LRMC requires many rows to estimate its factors and degrades when models are scarce: at
$n{=}10$ its MAE is $0.139$ versus $0.120$ for \PKPS (Figure~\ref{fig:completion},
dashed).
At the full cohort ($n{=}93$, solid), \PKPS slightly edges LRMC ($0.103$ vs.\ $0.105$)
and the ensemble performs best ($0.099$).

\paragraph{The completion gains are modest but consistent.}
Figure~\ref{fig:completion}e--h shows the per-missing-cell difference between LRMC's
and the ensemble's absolute errors, by dataset, cohort, coverage, and query depth.
The win is smaller than in the query-efficiency task but stays positive in the mean
across every lever: the response channel adds information wherever the score matrix
alone is rank-deficient, and never subtracts it.

\paragraph{The results are qualitatively similar on the Every Eval Ever suite.}
The second suite shows the same qualitative behavior with a larger completion margin
(lever sweeps in Appendix~\ref{app:eee}).
At $m{=}1$, \PKPS attains a third of the sample score's error ($0.076$ vs.\ $0.224$)
and matches the accuracy the sample score reaches at $m{=}8$ ($0.081$): an $8\times$
smaller budget.
On missing cells, the ensemble outperforms LRMC at every operating point, including the
full cohort ($0.069$ vs.\ $0.096$ at $n{=}45$).
Table~\ref{tab:suite} collects both tasks on both suites, and the ensemble is best or
tied in every column.

\paragraph{The results are insensitive to representation and pipeline choices.} Varying
the MDS dimension over $4$--$24$ moves \PKPS's MAE by less than $0.005$, and the
cross-validated bandwidth matches the best fixed choice (Appendix~\ref{app:sens}).
Ablations of the regression head, the response kernel, the ensemble weighting rule, and
the incidental query overlap in the EEE pools are in Appendix~\ref{app:abl}.

\section{Discussion}
In this paper we introduced the \PKPS, which compares models through their responses
to similar, rather than identical, queries.
Outside of curated leaderboards, evaluation data is typically unpaired: each model
answers the queries its evaluators chose, and methods that require a shared block of
identical queries do not apply.
Our central finding is that benchmark prediction remains possible in this regime: as
$\rho\!\to\!0$, DKPS and IRT degrade sharply while \PKPS does not.
The same representations support both tasks we studied---query-efficient evaluation and
(model, task) score completion---and the ensemble performs at least as well as either
component alone.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.} We close with the current limitations and the
extensions they suggest.
First, \PKPS embeds every observed response and query with a single fixed embedder.
We did not ablate this choice, and embedding functions are known to have a nonnegligible
effect at small budgets \citep{dkps_qeff}.
Second, the block-diagonal instantiation on the HELM suite disables bridging across
response domains.
A shared response space that lets responses in one domain inform another remains
untested.
Third, computing Eq.~\eqref{eq:A} is quadratic in the number of queries per model pair.
Fourth, the achievability part of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} is existential and provides no
rate in $n$, and strict domination at intermediate $\rho$ rests on some bandwidth
improving on exact matching, which we establish empirically rather than by a lower
bound.
Finally, the per-cell gains over score-only baselines are modest on dense low-rank
matrices and decisive in the sparse, noisy, unpaired regime that motivates the problem.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}
All methods are released as a documented Python package (the \PKPS estimator, matrix
completion, and IRT baselines), with the data-preparation, experiment, and figure scripts
for both the synthetic study and the HELM suite; every experiment is seeded, all real-data
numbers are means over $16$ seeds (synthetic: $50$), and hyperparameters are selected by the
leak-free cross-validation described in Section~\ref{sec:experiments} (leakage is checked by an
automated test that corrupts one model's held-out scores and verifies its predictions are
unchanged). Appendix~\ref{app:details} lists the suite composition, embedding models, and
all hyperparameter settings.

\paragraph{\PKPS.} RBF query kernel with bandwidth selected per run by leave-one-model-out
$k$-NN ($k{=}8$) on the observed scores over the grid
$\sigma\in\{0.03,0.1,0.3,1,3\}\times\mathrm{median}$ pairwise query distance, plus the
near-delta (DKPS) limit; linear response kernel on real data, RBF on synthetic. Response
blocks are capped at $48$ PCA components per dataset and the shared query space at $64$;
each (model, query) pair uses one sampled response ($r{=}1$). Models are embedded by
classical MDS with $d{=}8$ throughout, following \citet{dkps_qeff} and fixed a priori
($d$ = latent dimension on synthetic). Appendix~\ref{app:sens} shows the results are
insensitive to $d$ over $4$--$24$, and that the cross-validated bandwidth tracks the best
fixed choice.

\paragraph{Prediction head.} All embedding methods share one head: logit-transformed
scores, per-task standardization to unit variance, a two-way (model $+$ task) additive
bias, and a distance-weighted $k$-NN ($k{=}5$) regression of the residual on the model
embedding, fit \emph{leave-one-family-out}: all models from the target's developer are
excluded from its training set, so predictions never lean on near-duplicate siblings
\citep{dkps_qeff}. Held-out cells are predicted directly; observed cells leave their own
target out. The head is identical to the LRMC baseline's, so the two methods differ
only in how the residual is modeled ($k$-NN on the perspective versus a low-rank
factorization) and performance differences are attributable to the representation.
Matching the per-task standardization is important: without it, the model offset is
dominated by the tasks with the largest logit variance.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Low-rank matrix completion (LRMC) follows BenchPress \citep{benchpress}: logit
space, per-task standardization, two-way bias, rank-$2$ ALS with ridge $\lambda{=}0.1$,
averaged over $5$ random initializations; on observed cells it is cross-fit so a cell's
prediction never sees its own sample. IRT is a 1PL (Rasch) model on binary tasks: item
difficulties by maximum likelihood (L-BFGS) on the largest fully-observed
(model $\times$ item) block, per-model ability by bounded scalar MLE, clipped to
$[-4,4]$. The sample baseline is the cell's own $m$-query mean.

\paragraph{Ensemble.} On observed cells, the prediction is $\alpha\,\hat
y_{\mathrm{sample}}+(1-\alpha)\,\hat y_{\PKPS}$ with a scalar $\alpha$ per held-out
family, selected on a $21$-point grid by leave-one-family-out validation against the
other families' cached full scores (the same information the regressor trains on); on
missing cells, \PKPS and LRMC are blended with a weight fit by held-out cross-validation
against observed scores. No quantity ever uses the scores of the family being predicted.
Appendix~\ref{app:abl} compares this rule to a closed-form inverse-variance weighting
and to the fixed $m/M$ schedule of \citet{dkps_qeff}.

\paragraph{Sweeps and reporting.}\begin{sloppypar} All real-data sweeps use $16$ seeds; curves and table
entries report the per-seed mean over tasks, then the mean ($\pm1$ SEM) over seeds. Query
efficiency: budgets $m\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}$ at full cohort and coverage; cohort
$n\in\{10,20,40,60,93\}$ (HELM) or $\{10,20,30,45\}$ (EEE) and task coverage
$p_{\mathrm{task}}\in\{0.2,0.35,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$, both at the few-query point $m{=}2$.
Completion: task coverage as above and query depth
$p_{\mathrm{query}}\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0\}$ with cohort lines $n\in\{10,\text{full}\}$;
task count $T\in\{4,8,12,18\}$ (HELM) or $\{4,8,12,16\}$ (EEE); the cohort sweep holds
$p_{\mathrm{task}}{=}p_{\mathrm{query}}{=}0.5$.\end{sloppypar}

\paragraph{Synthetic study.} $50$ seeds per configuration; latent dimension $5$, observed
embedding dimension $20$, score noise $0.1$, response noise $0.5$; both kernels RBF;
$k$-NN regression with $k{=}5$. Panel-specific settings are given in each figure title.

\paragraph{Compute.} All experiments run on a single multi-core CPU node; text embeddings
are precomputed once through the embedding API and cached. Embedding the EEE suite
($30{,}304$ pooled responses plus $7{,}665$ queries, ${\sim}11$M tokens) took ${\sim}13$
minutes and under \$$2$ of API credit; each $16$-seed sweep completes in minutes (EEE) to
hours (HELM) on the same node.

\section{Sensitivity analyses}
\label{app:sens}

Figure~\ref{fig:sens} probes the two representation hyperparameters on the
query-efficiency experiment ($16$ seeds throughout). \textbf{MDS dimension (a, b).}
Repeating the budget sweep at $d\in\{4,8,24\}$ moves \PKPS's MAE by less than
$0.005$ at every budget on both suites (at the smallest budgets, smaller $d$ is very
slightly better---fewer coordinates to estimate from one noisy query per cell); the
paper's choice ($d{=}8$, following \citealp{dkps_qeff}) is never the worst point at any
budget. \textbf{Query bandwidth (c).} Fixing the
bandwidth at each grid multiplier instead of cross-validating traces the expected curve:
near-delta multipliers and the exact-matching limit ($k_Q(u,u')=\indone\{u=u'\}$,
abbreviated $\delta$) are substantially worse
(unpaired pools leave them little to compare), broad multipliers are best, and the
leak-free cross-validated choice matches the best fixed multiplier on both suites
($0.126$ vs.\ $0.125$ on HELM, $0.069$ vs.\ $0.068$ on EEE)---the one hyperparameter
that matters is the one the method selects automatically. Sensitivity to the number of
reference models is part of the main experiments (the cohort sweeps of
Figures~\ref{fig:qe}b and \ref{fig:completion}d).

\section{Pipeline ablations}
\label{app:abl}

Each ablation repeats the query-efficiency budget sweep ($16$ seeds, both suites)
with one pipeline choice changed; all other settings match Appendix~\ref{app:details}.

\paragraph{Ensemble weighting.} We compare the paper's cross-validated scalar weight
against two alternatives: a closed-form inverse-variance rule (the sample score weighted
by $e^2_{\PKPS}/(e^2_{\PKPS}+\tilde p(1-\tilde p)/m)$ per cell, with $\tilde p$ the
\PKPS estimate and $e^2_{\PKPS}$ the depth-weighted, noise-debiased disagreement with
the observed samples) and the fixed schedule $\alpha=m/M$ of \citet{dkps_qeff}. The
cross-validated weight is best at every budget on both suites: on HELM it improves on the
inverse-variance rule from $0.132$ to $0.125$ at $m{=}1$ and from $0.077$ to $0.073$ at
$m{=}8$; on EEE from $0.084$ to $0.073$ and from $0.052$ to $0.049$. The $m/M$ schedule
trails both on HELM ($0.096$ at $m{=}8$) and is close to the cross-validated weight on
EEE, where the small pools ($M{=}32$) let $m/M$ reach $1$.

\paragraph{Regression head.} Replacing the distance-weighted $k$-NN head with ordinary
least squares helps slightly on HELM ($0.132$ vs.\ $0.136$ at $m{=}1$; $0.080$ vs.\
$0.085$ at $m{=}32$), consistent with \citet{dkps_qeff}, but hurts on EEE, increasingly
so at large budgets ($0.066$ vs.\ $0.055$ at $m{=}32$). We use $k$-NN as the robust
default across suites.

\paragraph{Response kernel.} An RBF response kernel in place of the linear one changes
\PKPS's MAE by at most $0.003$ at every budget on both suites; the linear choice is
justified by the block-diagonal factorization (Appendix~\ref{app:details}) at no
accuracy cost.

\paragraph{Incidental overlap.} The EEE pools share ${\sim}10\%$ of items across
models by chance. Enforcing exactly disjoint query sets (true $\rho=0$) changes \PKPS
by at most $0.005$ at $m\le8$ ($0.080$ vs.\ $0.076$ at $m{=}1$), and IRT becomes
inapplicable outright---no shared item block exists. At $m\ge16$ the disjointness
constraint exhausts the $32$-item pools and mechanically shortens the sample score's
effective depth, so comparisons there reflect the constraint rather than the methods.

\section{Proofs of Theorems~\ref{prop:conc} and \ref{thm:qeff} and Corollary~\ref{cor:sample}}
\label{app:proof}

\paragraph{Formal definitions and assumptions.} We collect the formal statements referenced in Section~\ref{sec:method}.
\begin{definition}[Population \PKPS]
\label{def:pop}
Let each model $i$'s (task, query, response) triples be drawn i.i.d.\ from a design
distribution $P_i$. The \emph{population comparison} is
$A^*_{ii'}=\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')\,k_R(x_i,x_{i'})]\,/\,\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')]$ with draws from
$P_i$ and $P_{i'}$ independent, the population squared distances are
$D^{*2}(i,i')=A^*_{ii}+A^*_{i'i'}-2A^*_{ii'}$, and the \emph{population perspectives}
$\psi_1,\dots,\psi_n\in\mathbb{R}^d$ are the rank-$d$ spectral embedding of the centered
Gram matrix $B^*$ of $D^{*2}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{assumption}[Boundedness]\label{ass:bound}
Kernels and embedded responses are bounded.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Population overlap]\label{ass:overlap}
The \emph{cross-model query-kernel mass} is bounded below:
$\kappa=\inf_{i,i'}\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')]>0$.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Spectral identifiability]\label{ass:spec}
$B^*$ has rank $d$ with spectral gap $\lambda_d>0$.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Score regularity]\label{ass:score}
The benchmark score is bounded and $\gamma$-Lipschitz on the population perspective space.
\end{assumption}
\begin{assumption}[Model support]\label{ass:support}
The model distribution places mass on every neighborhood of the population perspective
space.
\end{assumption}

\paragraph{Population quantities.} Each of model $i$'s triples independently draws a
task, then a query conditioned on the task, then the model's response
(Section~\ref{sec:theory}); write $z=(u,x_i(u))$ for an embedded query--response
pair (response noise included) and $P_i$ for its law. By Assumption~\ref{ass:bound} the kernels are bounded:
$k_Q\in[0,1]$ after normalization (RBF) and $|k_R|\le B_R$. Define the population comparison
\[
A^*_{ii'}\;=\;\frac{\mathbb{E}\,[k_Q(u,u')\,k_R(x_i(u),x_{i'}(u'))]}{\mathbb{E}\,[k_Q(u,u')]},
\qquad z\sim P_i,\; z'\sim P_{i'} \text{ independent},
\]
population squared distances $D^{*2}(i,i')=A^*_{ii}+A^*_{i'i'}-2A^*_{ii'}$, the centered Gram
matrix $B^*=-\tfrac12 J D^{*2} J$ with $J=I-\tfrac1n\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$, and
population perspectives $\Psi^*\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ its rank-$d$ spectral embedding,
which exists with $\lambda_d(B^*)>0$ by Assumption~\ref{ass:spec} (cf.\ Definition~\ref{def:pop}).

\begin{lemma}[Affinity concentration]
\label{lem:conc}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:bound} and \ref{ass:overlap}, for models $i,i'$ with $\min(|Q_i|,|Q_{i'}|)\ge m$ and any
$\delta\in(0,1)$: with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\[
|A_{ii'}-A^*_{ii'}|\;\le\;\frac{2(1+B_R)}{\kappa^2}\,t_m(\delta),
\qquad t_m(\delta)=\bar B\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2m}},\quad \bar B=\max(2B_R,1),
\]
provided $t_m(\delta)\le\kappa/2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\begin{sloppypar}
The numerator $N=\frac{1}{|Q_i||Q_{i'}|}\sum_{j,l}k_Q(u_j,u'_l)\,k_R(x_{ij},x_{i'l})$ and
denominator $Z=\frac{1}{|Q_i||Q_{i'}|}\sum_{j,l}k_Q(u_j,u'_l)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:A} are
two-sample $U$-statistics of order $(1,1)$ in the i.i.d.\ pairs $z_j\sim P_i$,
$z'_l\sim P_{i'}$, with kernels of range at most $2B_R$ and $1$ respectively (response noise
enters only through the bounded kernel argument, so the number of responses per query may
stay fixed). By Hoeffding's inequality for two-sample $U$-statistics
\citep[Section~5]{hoeffding1963}, each deviates from its mean by more than its range times
$\sqrt{\log(4/\delta)/2m}$ with probability at most $\delta/2$; since $\bar B$ dominates
both ranges, a union bound gives $|N-N^*|\le t_m(\delta)$ and $|Z-Z^*|\le t_m(\delta)$
jointly with probability $\ge1-\delta$. On this event, since $Z^*\ge\kappa$ by Assumption~\ref{ass:overlap} and $t_m(\delta)\le\kappa/2$,
we have $Z\ge\kappa/2$, and
\[
|A_{ii'}-A^*_{ii'}|=\frac{|NZ^*-N^*Z|}{ZZ^*}
\;\le\;\frac{|N-N^*|}{Z}+\frac{|N^*|\,|Z-Z^*|}{ZZ^*}
\;\le\;\frac{2\,t_m(\delta)}{\kappa}+\frac{2B_R\,t_m(\delta)}{\kappa^2}
\;\le\;\frac{2(1+B_R)}{\kappa^2}\,t_m(\delta),
\]
using $|N^*|\le B_R$ and $\kappa\le1$.
\end{sloppypar}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Perspective perturbation]
\label{lem:pert}
Let $\varepsilon_\infty=\max_{i,i'}|A_{ii'}-A^*_{ii'}|$. There is an orthogonal
$W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ such that
\[
\max_i\;\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\;\le\;
\frac{25\sqrt{d}\;n\,\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}\,.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Entrywise, $|D^2-D^{*2}|\le4\varepsilon_\infty$, and double centering inflates an
entrywise bound by at most a factor of $4$, so $E=B-B^*$ satisfies
$\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le\|E\|_F\le8\,n\,\varepsilon_\infty$. Let $B_d$ be the top-$d$
truncation of $B$ that classical MDS embeds ($B_d=\hat\Psi\hat\Psi^\top$, positive
semidefinite). By Weyl's inequality every eigenvalue of $B$ beyond the $d$-th, and every
negative eigenvalue, has magnitude at most $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ (since
$\lambda_{d+1}(B^*)=0$ and $B^*\succeq0$), so
$\|B_d-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$. The matrix $B_d-B^*$ has rank at most
$2d$, hence
\[
\|B_d-B^*\|_F\;\le\;\sqrt{2d}\,\|B_d-B^*\|_{\mathrm{op}}
\;\le\;\sqrt{2d}\,\big(\|B_d-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}\big)
\;\le\;2\sqrt{2d}\,\|E\|_F .
\]
Both $B_d=\hat\Psi\hat\Psi^\top$ and $B^*=\Psi^*\Psi^{*\top}$ are positive semidefinite of
rank at most $d$, so by the Procrustes alignment lemma of
\citet[Lemma~5.4]{tu2016procrustes},
\[
\min_{W\in O(d)}\;\|\hat\Psi-\Psi^*W\|_F^2
\;\le\;\frac{\|B_d-B^*\|_F^2}{2(\sqrt2-1)\,\lambda_d(B^*)}\,.
\]
Combining, $\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le\|\hat\Psi-\Psi^*W\|_F\le
\frac{2\sqrt{2d}}{\sqrt{2(\sqrt2-1)}}\cdot\frac{8n\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}
\le\frac{25\sqrt d\,n\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}$. Nearest-neighbor
distances are invariant to $W$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Achievability with exact reference scores]
\label{lem:exact}
Suppose the reference scores are observed exactly ($\tilde{y}_{i}^{(t)}=y_{i}$).
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:bound}--\ref{ass:support}, for any $\epsilon>0$ there
exist a bandwidth $\sigma$ and $(n,m)$, with $m$ polynomial in
$(1/\epsilon,1/\kappa,n)$ and independent of $\rho$, at which
$\mathrm{MSE}(\PKPS_\sigma)\le\epsilon$ with high probability.
\end{lemma}
\paragraph{Proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:exact}.} Take scores in $[0,1]$ without loss of
generality (Assumption~\ref{ass:score}). Fix $\epsilon>0$ and set targets $c\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}/(8\gamma)$ for
the estimation error and $\epsilon'\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}/(2\gamma)$ for the population
nearest-neighbor distance. By Assumption~\ref{ass:support} there is $n_0(\epsilon')$ such that for $n\ge n_0$, a
reference model within population distance $\epsilon'$ of the target exists except with
probability at most $\epsilon/8$. Fix such an $n$. Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:conc} with
confidence $\delta=\epsilon/(8n^2)$ to every pair, a union bound and
Lemma~\ref{lem:pert} give $\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le c$ except with probability at
most $\epsilon/8$, once
\[
m\;\ge\;C'\,\frac{d\,(1+B_R)^2\,\bar B^2\,n^2\log(n/\epsilon)}{\kappa^4\,\lambda_d\,c^2}
\;=\;\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\kappa,1/\epsilon),
\]
for an absolute constant $C'$ (this choice also satisfies the proviso
$t_m\le\kappa/2$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:conc}), and does not involve the pairing fraction
$\rho$. On the intersection of the two
events, for a target model $f$ with nearest estimated reference neighbor $f^*$
(perspectives $\psi^*,\hat\psi^*$; the target's $\psi,\hat\psi$),
\[
|\hat y(f)-y(f)|\;\le\;\gamma\,\|\psi^*-\psi\|\;\le\;
\gamma\big(\|\hat\psi^*-W\psi^*\|+\|\hat\psi^*-\hat\psi\|+\|\hat\psi-W\psi\|\big)
\;\le\;\gamma\,(2c+\hat\delta),
\]
using Assumption~\ref{ass:score} and orthogonal invariance. The estimated nearest-neighbor distance
$\hat\delta$ is at most the estimated distance to the $\epsilon'$-close reference, itself
at most $\epsilon'+2c$ by the same alignment argument, so
$|\hat y-y|\le\gamma(4c+\epsilon')\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}$. Squaring, and adding the two
failure events (squared errors are bounded by $1$),
$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat y)\le\epsilon/2+\epsilon/4\le\epsilon$. \hfill$\qed$

\paragraph{Relative to the sample score.} The sample score at budget $m$ is the mean of
$m$ i.i.d.\ per-query scores and uses no reference models, so its MSE is $\sigma_q^2/m$
for every $n$, where $\sigma_q^2$ is the per-query score variance ($\le1/4$ for scores
in $[0,1]$). For any $\epsilon<\sigma_q^2/m$ at the budget of
Lemma~\ref{lem:exact}, the estimator satisfies
$\mathrm{MSE}\le\epsilon<\sigma_q^2/m$ for all sufficiently large $n$: query efficiency
relative to the sample score in the sense of Definition~\ref{def:qeff}, proving
Corollary~\ref{cor:sample}.

\paragraph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}: noisy reference scores.} Write the scoring function as
$s^{(t)}(f,A)=\tfrac{1}{|A|}\sum_{q\in A}r(f,q)$ with per-query scores
$r\in[0,1]$, and let each reference cell $Q_{i}^{(t)}$ be a uniform subsample of
$Q^{(t)}$ of size $m$. Then
$\mathbb{E}[\tilde{y}_{i}^{(t)}\mid f_{i}]=y_{i}$ and
$\mathrm{Var}(\tilde{y}_{i}^{(t)}\mid f_{i})\le\sigma_q^2/m$, and the noises
$\varepsilon_{i}=\tilde{y}_{i}^{(t)}-y_{i}$ are independent across $i$ because
the subsamples are drawn independently (unpaired designs). For the $k$-nearest-neighbor regressor, decompose
$\hat y(f)-y=\tfrac1k\sum_{j\in N_k}(y_{j}-y)+\tfrac1k\sum_{j\in
N_k}\varepsilon_{j}$ over the neighbor set $N_k$. The first term is bounded as in
the proof of Lemma~\ref{lem:exact}: perspective estimation error
(Theorem~\ref{prop:conc}) plus the population $k$-th-neighbor distance, which
Assumption~\ref{ass:support} makes small for $n$ large with $k/n\to0$, times the
Lipschitz constant. The second term is a mean of $k$ independent, bounded,
mean-zero variables, so its conditional variance is at most $\sigma_q^2/(mk)$ and
it concentrates by Hoeffding. Choosing $k\to\infty$ with $k/n\to0$ (e.g.,
$k=\lceil\log n\rceil$) gives
$\mathrm{MSE}\le\epsilon+\sigma_q^2/(mk)\to\epsilon$. Under a common
(paired) subsample the $\varepsilon_{i}$ share the draw of the query set and
$\mathrm{Cov}(\varepsilon_{i},\varepsilon_{j})$ equals the variance of the
common query-difficulty component, which the neighbor average does not reduce.
\qed

\paragraph{Contrast with paired DKPS.} With the exact-matching kernel $k_Q=\delta$
($k_Q(u,u')=\indone\{u=u'\}$), only identical query pairs enter the double sum, so the
$U$-statistic runs over the $\rho m$ shared queries
and the rate in Lemma~\ref{lem:conc} becomes $O_P\big((\rho m)^{-1/2}\big)$: DKPS's
guarantee requires $\rho m\to\infty$ and vacates in the unpaired limit $\rho\to0$,
where its distance is undefined (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\paragraph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}: domination and strict separation.} Existence of a dominating bandwidth is
witnessed by the exact-matching member: the $k_Q=\delta$ estimator coincides with
paired DKPS on the shared queries with the same regression head
(Section~\ref{sec:method}), so
$\sigma=\delta$ attains $\mathrm{MSE}(\PKPS_\sigma)=\mathrm{MSE}(\mathrm{DKPS})$ at
every $(n,m)$, and any bandwidth improving on exact matching witnesses strict
domination. The remainder establishes the operational form: cross-validation attains
the best grid member up to $\epsilon$. Split the reference models into a training
set $T$ and a validation set $V$ with $|V|=n_v$. For each $\sigma\in\Sigma$, build the
perspective space on $T$, embed any model outside $T$ out of sample by its
Eq.~\eqref{eq:A} comparison row to $T$ (the standard out-of-sample MDS coordinate), and let
$\hat y_\sigma$ be nearest-neighbor regression on $T$'s (perspective, score) pairs. The
selected bandwidth is $\hat\sigma=\arg\min_{\sigma\in\Sigma}\hat L(\sigma)$ with
$\hat L(\sigma)=\tfrac1{n_v}\sum_{i\in V}(\hat y_\sigma(f_i)-y_i)^2$. Conditional on
$T$, the validation losses are i.i.d.\ across the draw of validation models and bounded
in $[0,1]$, so by Hoeffding's inequality and a union bound over $\Sigma$, with
probability at least $1-\delta'$,
$\sup_{\sigma\in\Sigma}|\hat L(\sigma)-L(\sigma)|\le
\tau=\sqrt{\log(2|\Sigma|/\delta')/2n_v}$, where $L(\sigma)$ is the MSE on a fresh
target. On this event,
\[
L(\hat\sigma)\;\le\;\hat L(\hat\sigma)+\tau\;\le\;\hat L(\delta)+\tau
\;\le\;L(\delta)+2\tau\;=\;\mathrm{MSE}(\mathrm{DKPS})+2\tau,
\]
since the $k_Q=\delta$ member of the family coincides with paired DKPS on the shared
queries with the same regression head. Taking $n_v$ large enough that
$2\tau\le\epsilon$ transfers the domination guarantee to the cross-validated
estimator at every $(m,\rho)$. If some
$\sigma^\star$ has $\Delta=L(\delta)-L(\sigma^\star)>0$, the same event gives
$L(\hat\sigma)\le L(\sigma^\star)+2\tau=\mathrm{MSE}(\mathrm{DKPS})-\Delta+2\tau$,
strict for $2\tau<\Delta$. Finally, at $\rho=0$ with nonatomic designs,
$\Pr(q_j=q'_l)=0$ for every pair of independently drawn queries, so almost surely every
identity-matched sum is empty: any measurable completion of the DKPS member predicts as a
function of the reference data alone, independent of the target, whence
$\mathrm{MSE}(\mathrm{DKPS})=v+\mathbb{E}[(\mathbb{E}y-\hat y)^2]\ge v$, while under
Assumptions~\ref{ass:bound}--\ref{ass:support} the achievability argument
supplies a bandwidth
attaining $\mathrm{MSE}\le\epsilon$, so $\Delta\ge v-\epsilon$ and the margin is at
least $v-\epsilon-2\tau\ge v-2\epsilon$. In practice we use leave-one-model-out
validation and a joint embedding (Section~\ref{sec:experiments}); the split protocol is
analyzed for independence of the validation losses. \hfill$\qed$
