\author{Anonymous}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Evaluating a generative model on all available benchmarks is often prohibitively
expensive. As such, models are often evaluated on different subsets of tasks and/or different subsets of
queries from a given task, and the resulting (model $\times$ task) score matrix is sparse
and noisy. In this paper, we introduce the \textbf{Product Kernel Perspective Space
(\PKPS)}---a collection of low-dimensional representations of models based on their
embedded responses---that generalizes existing benchmark-prediction methods to the sparse
and noisy evaluation regime by comparing responses to similar, rather than identical,
queries. Theoretically, we show that \PKPS more effectively uses information from unpaired
queries for a given task. Empirically, we show that \PKPS outperforms sample means,
item-response theory, and paired methods where evaluation is sparse, noisy, or unpaired,
and that it is a complementary signal to score-based methods.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Generative models are evaluated on an ever-growing collection of benchmarks spanning
knowledge, reasoning, code, translation, and safety \citep{helm}. Evaluating a model on
all of them is often prohibitively expensive. As a result, models are evaluated on different
subsets of tasks and on different subsets of queries within a task: a recent compilation of $84$ frontier models on $133$ benchmarks
finds only $23\%$ of the score matrix filled \citep{benchpress}. This sparsity is not an
artifact of curation; it is a consequence of how evaluations are collected. Arena-style
platforms score each model on the conversations its users generate \citep{chiang2024chatbot};
contamination-aware benchmarks refresh their items, so models evaluated in different
months answer different questions \citep{white2024livebench}; and cost-driven subsampling
gives each evaluation run its own item subset \citep{perlitz2024efficient}. The resulting
(model $\times$ task) score matrix is sparse, its entries are noisy averages over few
queries, and the responses beneath it are \emph{unpaired}: two models rarely answer the
same queries. Predicting the missing and true entries of this matrix would let
practitioners reuse cached evaluations instead of paying to re-run them
\citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks, vivek2024anchor, benchpress}.

Two lines of work aim to reduce this cost by predicting scores rather than computing
them. Efficient benchmarking extrapolates a task's full score from a small scored subset of its
items, classically by item-response theory \citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks}; matrix
completion imputes cells that were never run, from low-rank structure across the matrix
\citep{benchpress}. These are two ends of the same problem: every cell of the
score matrix is observed with some number of queries, possibly zero, and predicting its
true score amounts to denoising when that number is small and to completion when it is
zero. A single representation of each model that is accurate enough to predict any cell
would serve both ends.

Neither line of work has such a representation: both use only each cell's aggregate
score, and the responses that produced those scores carry more information than the
scores themselves. The Data Kernel Perspective Space (DKPS) \citep{dkps_qeff} uses this
information: it embeds each model from its responses via classical multidimensional
scaling and regresses benchmark scores in the resulting space. The DKPS distance between
two models, however, is defined only over responses to shared queries---a \emph{paired}
design---and is undefined when models were evaluated on different queries. To our
knowledge, no existing method predicts the score matrix from evaluation data as it is
typically available: sparse, noisy, and unpaired.

In this paper, we introduce the \textbf{Product Kernel Perspective Space (\PKPS)}, a
collection of low-dimensional representations of models based on their embedded responses.
\PKPS compares responses to \emph{similar}---rather than only identical---queries, so
every unpaired response contributes and paired DKPS is recovered as a special case
(Section~\ref{sec:method}). Theoretically, we show that \PKPS is query-efficient
relative to both the sample score---with a budget that does not depend on the fraction
of queries shared across models---and, under fully unpaired designs, paired DKPS
(Theorems~\ref{thm:qeff} and~\ref{thm:sep}). Empirically, we demonstrate on two heterogeneous suites---$93$
models on HELM tasks and $45$ modern models from the Every Eval Ever datastore
\citep{eee2026}---that \PKPS achieves lower error than sample means, item-response
theory, and DKPS in sparse, noisy, and unpaired regimes, matching the accuracy of sample
means with an $8\times$ smaller query budget, and that ensembling it with score-based
methods reduces the error of low-rank matrix completion by up to $28\%$
(Section~\ref{sec:experiments}).

\subsection{Problem setting and notation}
\label{sec:setting}
A \emph{model} is a random mapping $f:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{X}$ from a query space to a
response space; querying $f$ at $q$ returns a draw from the distribution $f(q)$. We
observe $n$ models $f_1,\dots,f_n$, each run on its own \emph{query set}
$Q_i\subseteq\mathcal{Q}$; the sets may overlap arbitrarily, and the paired design
$Q_1=\dots=Q_n$ is a special case. Two fixed embedding functions map raw objects to
vectors: a \emph{response} embedding $\mathrm{embed}_R:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}$ and
a \emph{query} embedding $\mathrm{embed}_Q:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{p'}$. For
$q_j\in Q_i$ we write
$x_{ij}=\tfrac1r\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mathrm{embed}_R\!\big(f_i(q_j)^{(k)}\big)$ for the
average of the $r$ sampled response embeddings and $u_j=\mathrm{embed}_Q(q_j)$ for the
embedded query.

Queries are grouped into $T$ tasks. Model $i$'s \emph{true score} on task $t$, $y_{it}$,
is its mean score over the task's full query set; running the model on $m_{it}\ge0$ of
those queries yields an observed \emph{sample score} whose noise scales as
$1/\sqrt{m_{it}}$. The goal is to predict $y_{it}$ for every cell of the
(model $\times$ task) matrix: cells observed with few queries ($m_{it}>0$;
\emph{denoising}, the efficient-benchmarking end) and cells never run ($m_{it}=0$;
\emph{completion}). We write $y_i$ when the benchmark has a single score. Two properties
of the design recur throughout: \emph{coverage}, the fraction of cells with $m_{it}>0$,
and \emph{pairing}, the fraction $\rho$ of a cell's queries shared across models; real
evaluations sit near $\rho\approx0$.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Low-dimensional representations of models.} Some approaches construct
representations by comparing internal activations across models
\citep{duderstadt2023comparing, huh2024platonic, horwitz2025atlas} or by comparing model
weights directly \citep{chen2025weights}. Evaluation frameworks, however, typically permit
only black-box access: they observe responses, not internals. The Data Kernel Perspective
Space (DKPS), first introduced in the context of monitoring multi-agent systems
\citep{helm2024tracking}, addresses this setting by mapping a collection of models into a
low-dimensional Euclidean space via classical multidimensional scaling of average embedded
responses \citep{torgerson1952mds}. Prior work established consistency and concentration
of the representations \citep{acharyya2024consistent, acharyya2025concentration},
supervised inference thereon \citep{helm2025inference}, and query efficiency
\citep{dkps_qeff}. These constructions compare responses to shared queries, a paired
design. \PKPS replaces the identity matching with a product kernel so that models
evaluated on different queries remain comparable.

\paragraph{Benchmark prediction.} A second line of work predicts unobserved (model, task)
scores so that not every evaluation must be run; efficient benchmarking and matrix
completion are its two ends. \citet{polo2024tinybenchmarks} use item-response theory to
construct small item subsets whose scores predict full performance \citep[see
also][]{rasch1960, metabench}; \citet{vivek2024anchor} cluster items by cross-model
agreement and score only the cluster centers; \citet{bean2025scales} select items using
cognitive-scale embeddings; \citet{li2024active} select items adaptively via reinforcement
learning; and \citet{perlitz2024efficient} analyze the cost--reliability tradeoff of
benchmark designs. Relatedly, \citet{ilyas2022datamodels} predict a model's outputs as a
function of its training data, \citet{prabhu2024lifelong} reuse past evaluations in
lifelong benchmarks, and \citet{dkps_qeff} predict held-out scores from cached responses.
On the completion end, \citet{benchpress} observe that the (model $\times$ task) score
matrix is approximately rank-two and complete it in logit space \citep[see
also][]{candes2009exact, mazumder2010softimpute, koren2009matrix}. These methods use only
each cell's aggregate score. \PKPS unifies the two ends---a cell with $m\ge0$ queries
spans efficient benchmarking ($m{>}0$) and completion ($m{=}0$)---and adds a response-level
signal that can be ensembled with matrix completion.

\section{The Product Kernel Perspective Space}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Models are embedded by classical MDS of pairwise affinities.} Each method
summarizes the $n$ models of
Section~\ref{sec:setting} in an $n\times n$ affinity matrix $A$ (defined below). The squared distance between two models is
$D^2(a,b)=A_{aa}+A_{bb}-2A_{ab}$ (clamped at $0$), and their $d$-dimensional
\emph{representations} are the classical-multidimensional-scaling minimizer
\begin{equation}
(\hat\psi_1,\dots,\hat\psi_n)=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{z_1,\dots,z_n\in\mathbb{R}^d}\
\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\big(\|z_i-z_k\|-D(i,k)\big)^2,
\label{eq:mds}
\end{equation}
so model $i$ is the point $\hat\psi_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$, its \emph{perspective}. Paired DKPS
and \PKPS differ only in the affinity $A$.

\paragraph{Paired DKPS compares responses to identical queries only.} With all $n$
models answering the same $m$ queries
($Q_1=\dots=Q_n$), DKPS uses the identity-matched affinity
$A_{ab}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_j k_R(x_{aj},x_{bj})$: response $j$ of model $a$ is paired only
with response $j$ of model $b$, so the design must be paired.

\paragraph{\PKPS weights every response comparison by query similarity.} \PKPS replaces
identity matching with a query kernel $k_Q$:
\begin{equation}
A_{ab} = \frac{\sum_{j\in Q_a}\sum_{l\in Q_b} k_Q(q_j,q_l)\, k_R(x_{aj},x_{bl})}
              {\sum_{j\in Q_a}\sum_{l\in Q_b} k_Q(q_j,q_l)},
\label{eq:A}
\end{equation}
where $Q_a$ is the query set of model $a$. Each term is normalized by its own query-kernel
mass, so models that answered different numbers of (or entirely different) queries are
still comparable; responses to \emph{similar} queries---even across tasks---contribute and
unpaired evaluations are used (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}). Paired DKPS is the special case
$k_Q=\delta$, where only $j=l$ survives.

\paragraph{An RBF query kernel; a linear or RBF response kernel.} The response kernel
$k_R$ compares embedded responses, the query
kernel $k_Q$ compares embedded queries. Paired DKPS uses a linear response kernel and the
identity query kernel $k_Q=\delta$. We use an RBF query kernel over the embedded queries,
$k_Q(q_j,q_l)=\exp(-\|u_j-u_l\|^2/2\sigma^2)$, with bandwidth $\sigma$ chosen per run
(below); $k_R$ is RBF in the synthetic study and linear on real data, where linearity makes
the kernel factorize across response domains (Section~\ref{sec:setup}). Since Eq.~\ref{eq:A}
normalizes each pair by its own kernel mass, $A$ need not be positive semidefinite;
classical MDS embeds the doubly-centered matrix by its top-$d$ nonnegative eigenvalues, as
is standard. $A_{ab}$ costs $|Q_a||Q_b|$ kernel terms per pair, and one response-kernel
pass serves the whole bandwidth grid.

\subsection{Theoretical properties of the \PKPS}
\PKPS is a strict generalization of DKPS. With $k_Q=\delta$ only equal queries lying in
both query sets survive the double sum of Eq.~\ref{eq:A}, so it reduces to paired DKPS
over the shared queries,
$A_{ab}=\tfrac{1}{|Q_a\cap Q_b|}\sum_{q\in Q_a\cap Q_b}k_R(x_{aq},x_{bq})$; since the RBF
kernel converges pointwise to $\delta$ as $\sigma\!\to\!0$, DKPS is also the
small-bandwidth limit. Conversely, for any strictly positive $k_Q$ the normalizer is
positive for any nonempty query sets, so $A_{ab}$ is defined even on disjoint sets, where
paired DKPS is undefined; between the limits, $\sigma$ trades exact matching for
bridging.

\subsection{Inference in the \PKPS}
Inference is a two-step statistical problem, exactly as in the DKPS \citep{dkps_qeff}:
embed each model into the perspective space by the scaling of Eq.~\ref{eq:mds}, then
regress its benchmark score on the resulting coordinate. On reference models with known
scores $\{(f_i,y_i)\}$ we fit $\hat h:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathcal{Y}$ on the pairs
$\{(\hat\psi_i,y_i)\}$ and predict $\hat y(f)=\hat h(\hat\psi(f))$. The free choices are
the bandwidth $\sigma$ and the regressor $\hat h$.

\paragraph{Leak-free cross-validation chooses the bandwidth.} The optimal $\sigma$
depends on how paired the data is: small when query overlap is high, large when it is low.
We choose it per run by cross-validation: over a median-scaled grid
$\sigma\in\{0.03,\dots,3\}\cdot\mathrm{med}$, select the $\sigma$ whose perspective best
predicts the \emph{observed} scores under leave-one-model-out $k$-NN. The criterion never
sees the held-out scores we report. And because the grid reaches near-delta,
cross-validation \emph{recovers DKPS} when overlap is high and bridges only when it must.

\paragraph{The estimation pipeline shares the matrix-completion head.} For each
replicate we (i) PCA-reduce response and query embeddings on the \emph{observed} rows
only (dimension by the second Zhu--Ghodsi elbow); (ii) form $D$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:A};
(iii) embed models by classical MDS; (iv) regress scores on the embedding, fit
leave-one-family-out. The score head---logit transform, per-task standardization, two-way
(model $+$ task) bias---is identical to the low-rank matrix-completion (LRMC) baseline's; the two methods
differ only in the factor, $k$-NN on the perspective versus a low-rank factorization, so
comparisons isolate the representation. Matching the per-task standardization matters:
without it the model offset is dominated by the highest-logit-variance tasks.

\paragraph{The ensemble folds in whichever score signal a cell has.} The rule is
precision weighting: trust each channel in proportion to its estimated reliability. An
\emph{observed} cell's score signal is its own sample mean, shrunk toward the \PKPS
estimate by the weight $\sigma_q^2/(\sigma_q^2+e_{\PKPS}^2)$---sample noise variance over
total disagreement with \PKPS---so the sample dominates as queries grow and the embedding
as noise grows. A \emph{missing} cell's score signal is LRMC
\citep{benchpress}, blended with \PKPS by a weight cross-validated on observed scores.
Every weight is read off observed queries alone ($\hat p(1-\hat p)/m$; depth-weighted
disagreement); none touches the held-out scores we report.

\subsection{Query efficiency}
\label{sec:qeff}
DKPS requires query \emph{sets} to overlap; \PKPS requires only query
\emph{distributions} to overlap. This section makes that distinction precise by extending
the machinery of \citet{dkps_qeff}.
\begin{definition}[Population \PKPS]
\label{def:pop}
Let each model $a$'s (query, response) pairs be drawn i.i.d.\ from a design distribution
$P_a$. The \emph{population affinity} is
$A^*_{ab}=\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')\,k_R(x_a,x_b)]\,/\,\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')]$ with draws from
$P_a$ and $P_b$ independent, the population squared distances are
$D^{*2}(a,b)=A^*_{aa}+A^*_{bb}-2A^*_{ab}$, and the \emph{population perspectives}
$\psi_1,\dots,\psi_n\in\mathbb{R}^d$ are the rank-$d$ spectral embedding of the centered
Gram matrix $B^*$ of $D^{*2}$.
\end{definition}
\begin{assumption}[Score regularity]\label{ass:score}
The benchmark score is bounded and $\gamma$-Lipschitz on the population perspective space.
\end{assumption}
In practice, Assumption~\ref{ass:score} holds when models with similar response
distributions attain similar scores---the premise of any response-based predictor, and
visible as the smooth score gradient in Figure~\ref{fig:concept}(e).
\begin{assumption}[Model support]\label{ass:support}
The model distribution places mass on every neighborhood of the population perspective
space.
\end{assumption}
Assumption~\ref{ass:support} ensures that a target model has arbitrarily near reference
neighbors once the cohort is large; in practice it suggests that a large, diverse
reference population may be needed. Both conditions mirror \citet{dkps_qeff}.
\begin{assumption}[Kernel and geometry regularity]\label{ass:reg}
Kernels and embedded responses are bounded; the \emph{cross-model query-kernel mass} is
bounded below, $\kappa=\inf_{a,b}\mathbb{E}[k_Q(u,u')]>0$; and $B^*$ has rank $d$ with
spectral gap $\lambda_d>0$.
\end{assumption}
Boundedness and the spectral gap are standard in spectral-embedding analyses. The
substantive condition is $\kappa>0$: it is the population analogue of the pairing
fraction $\rho$, and for an RBF $k_Q$ it holds whenever query \emph{distributions}
overlap, even when no individual query is shared. It fails only when query distributions
are disjoint---models evaluated on domains the kernel cannot bridge---in which case no
unpaired method should be expected to compare them.
\begin{proposition}[Concentration of \PKPS perspectives]
\label{prop:conc}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:reg}, with at least $m$ queries per model, for any
$\delta\in(0,1)$, with probability at least $1-\delta$ there is an orthogonal $W$ such
that
$\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le
C\kappa^{-2}\lambda_d^{-1/2}\sqrt{d}\,n\sqrt{\log(4n^2/\delta)/2m}$,
where $C$ depends only on the kernel bounds (proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}).
\end{proposition}
\begin{definition}[Query efficiency; following \citealp{dkps_qeff}]
\label{def:qeff}
Fix a per-model budget $m$ and let $h_n$ denote a score estimator using $n$ scored
reference models. $h$ is \emph{query-efficient relative to} $h'$ \emph{at budget} $m$ if
there exists $N$ such that $\mathrm{MSE}(h_n)\le\mathrm{MSE}(h'_n)$ with high
probability for all $n\ge N$, and \emph{query-efficient relative to} $h'$ if this holds
at every budget at which $\mathrm{MSE}(h'_n)$ is bounded away from zero.
\end{definition}
\begin{theorem}[Query efficiency of \PKPS]
\label{thm:qeff}
Under Assumptions~\ref{ass:score}--\ref{ass:reg}, for any $\epsilon>0$ there exist
$(n,m)$, with $m$ polynomial in $(1/\epsilon,1/\kappa,n)$ and independent of the pairing
fraction $\rho$, such that nearest-neighbor regression in the \PKPS attains mean squared
error at most $\epsilon$ with high probability. That is, whenever the sample score's
error at that budget remains above $\epsilon$---its MSE is $\sigma_q^2/m$ for every $n$,
where $\sigma_q^2$ is the per-query score variance---\PKPS is query-efficient relative
to the sample score, at every pairing fraction including $\rho=0$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
Proposition~\ref{prop:conc} bounds the estimation error of every perspective. By
Assumption~\ref{ass:score} and the triangle inequality, the nearest-neighbor regression
error splits into two estimation terms and the population nearest-neighbor distance, which
Assumption~\ref{ass:support} makes small for $n$ large; choosing
$m=\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\kappa,1/\epsilon)$ gives $\mathrm{MSE}\le\epsilon$, and no step
involves $\rho$. The sample score uses no reference models, so its error at budget $m$
is fixed at $\sigma_q^2/m$; attaining $\epsilon$ below it gives the relative statement.
Full proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}.
\end{proof}
Applied to paired DKPS ($k_Q=\delta$), the same argument retains only the $\rho M_{ij}$
shared query pairs, so the DKPS bound's effective budget is $\rho m$ and its guarantee
degrades as $\rho\to0$ while Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} does not. Under fully unpaired
designs the comparison closes (proof in Appendix~\ref{app:proof}):
\begin{theorem}[Separation under unpaired designs]
\label{thm:sep}
Assume additionally that scores are non-degenerate, $v=\mathrm{Var}(y)>0$, and that the
design distributions are nonatomic, so that no two models share a query almost surely.
Then every paired-DKPS estimator has $\mathrm{MSE}\ge v$ at every $(n,m)$, while
Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} attains any $\epsilon<v$: \PKPS is query-efficient relative to
paired DKPS (Definition~\ref{def:qeff}).
\end{theorem}
For intermediate pairing, the gap widens empirically as $\rho\to0$
(Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic}e); a rate in $\rho$ is open.

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}
We evaluate \PKPS on synthetic data, where every lever of the observation process can be
swept, and on two heterogeneous benchmark suites. Throughout, the two prediction problems
of Section~\ref{sec:setting} structure the experiments: denoising observed cells (query
efficiency) and predicting missing ones (completion).

\subsection{Evaluation setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Synthetic data.} We generate $n$ models with latent ability vectors $v_i$ and
$T$ tasks with latent vectors $t_k$, scores $v_i\cdot t_k+\epsilon$, and queries drawn
near each task center. Each model answers $M_{ij}$ queries per task, of which $m_{ii'}$
are shared with another model (overlap $\rho=m_{ii'}/M_{ij}$); a task is observed with
probability $p_{\mathrm{task}}$ and a query with probability $p_{\mathrm{query}}$.

\paragraph{Benchmark suites.} The first suite is $18$ tasks across four HELM datasets
with very different response types---MATH ($7$ math subjects), WMT-14 ($5$ translation
directions), MedQA (medical multiple-choice), and LegalBench ($5$ legal classification
subsets)---on the $93$ models evaluated on all four. MATH and WMT have rich free-text
responses; MedQA and LegalBench responses are single tokens for which text embeddings are
degenerate, so we encode each dataset's responses natively (Gemini embeddings vs.\ one-hot
answers) in disjoint blocks. With a linear $k_R$ this makes cross-dataset response
similarity exactly zero, and a single shared query embedding with a within-domain
bandwidth keeps $k_Q$ near zero across domains: the product kernel factorizes by domain,
never comparing a proof to a legal label. The second suite, from the Every Eval Ever
datastore \citep{eee2026}, is $45$ modern models on $16$ tasks (MATH-MC levels $1$--$5$,
GSM-MC, GPQA-Diamond, and nine judging categories from JudgeBench and RewardBench~2), with
per-cell query pools drawn independently per model ($\sim\!10\%$ incidental
overlap)---unpaired by construction (Appendix~\ref{app:eee}).

\paragraph{Prediction methods.} We compare six predictors. The \emph{sample mean} is a
cell's own $m$-query average. \emph{IRT} fits a one-parameter logistic model on the
largest shared item block \citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks}. \emph{Low-rank matrix
completion} (LRMC) is the logit-space imputation of \citet{benchpress} (missing cells only). \emph{DKPS}
and \emph{\PKPS} regress scores on the model embeddings of Section~\ref{sec:method},
DKPS being the identity-query-kernel limit. The \emph{ensemble} combines \PKPS with
whichever score signal a cell has (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\paragraph{Measurement.} Every experiment predicts held-out or true cell scores and
reports MAE against the full-evaluation score, mean $\pm1$ SEM over $16$ seeds ($50$ on
synthetic data). Sweeps vary one observation lever at a time, holding the others fixed
(grids in Appendix~\ref{app:details}); all hyperparameters, including the query
bandwidth, are chosen per run by the leak-free cross-validation of
Section~\ref{sec:method}.

\subsection{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\paragraph{\PKPS outperforms DKPS on every observation lever.} We predict held-out
(model, task) scores against the score-only sample baseline (Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic}).
\PKPS beats DKPS on \emph{every} lever, and both beat the sample floor: bridging similar
queries---across tasks as well as within---supplies signal that exact matching cannot
reach, so the margin is wide even when the data is mostly paired (a--c). The gap peaks where pairing is scarce:
as $p_{\mathrm{query}}$ drops (d) DKPS degrades while \PKPS stays flat, and sweeping
$\rho$ directly (e), DKPS collapses toward the floor as $\rho\!\to\!0$ while \PKPS is
flat---it does not rely on pairing.

Panel (f) is the \emph{denoising} form of the same problem: each cell is now \emph{observed}
with $M_{ij}$ noisy queries (sample noise $\propto1/\sqrt{M_{ij}}$) and we predict its true
score. The raw sample mean falls like $1/\sqrt{M_{ij}}$; smoothing those noisy scores over the
perspective denoises well below it. \PKPS at one query matches the sample at roughly
five, paired or unpaired; DKPS denoises only when paired (dashed). Only at large budgets
is the raw sample precise enough to overtake the embedding's residual bias. This is the empirical counterpart of
Theorems~\ref{thm:qeff} and~\ref{thm:sep}.

\paragraph{\PKPS-based methods outperform the sample score at small query budgets.}
Each model answers $m$ queries per cell, sampled independently---the real-data
$\rho\!\to\!0$ regime, on which IRT is poorly identified---and we predict the true
full-eval score (Figure~\ref{fig:qe}). The embedding captures response \emph{style},

better channel throughout. Panels (b, c) separate the embeddings: \PKPS improves steadily with cohort

\paragraph{The embedding completes missing cells where LRMC cannot.}
The same embedding also predicts cells that
were \emph{never run}. We observe a fraction $p_{\mathrm{task}}$ of cells (each at depth
$p_{\mathrm{query}}$) and predict the missing ones, against the LRMC
baseline (the sample does not apply---a missing cell has no sample). Low-rank completion needs
many rows: when models are scarce it fails while the embedding remains accurate---at
$n{=}10$, LRMC is $0.139$ versus \PKPS's $0.120$ (Figure~\ref{fig:completion},
dashed). At full cohort ($n{=}93$,
solid) \PKPS slightly edges LRMC ($0.103$ vs.\ $0.105$) and the ensemble is best
($0.099$).

\paragraph{The per-cell wins are broad but tail-driven.} The ensemble beats the raw
sample on most cells, with the largest gains at the smallest budgets and on the
rich-response datasets (Figure~\ref{fig:qe}d--g); the win over LRMC is modest but
stays positive in the mean across datasets, cohorts, coverage, and query depth
(Figure~\ref{fig:completion}e--h).

\paragraph{Both applications replicate on the Every Eval Ever suite.} The second suite
sharpens the completion contrast (Table~\ref{tab:suite}; lever sweeps in
Appendix~\ref{app:eee}): at $m{=}1$ \PKPS attains a third of the sample's error ($0.076$
vs.\ $0.224$), and on missing cells the ensemble beats LRMC at \emph{every}
operating point, including full cohort ($0.069$ vs.\ $0.096$ at $n{=}45$)---this
higher-rank matrix favors the response channel. Table~\ref{tab:suite} collects both
applications on both suites; the ensemble is best or tied nearly everywhere.

\paragraph{The results are insensitive to representation hyperparameters.} Varying the
MDS dimension over $4$--$24$ moves \PKPS's MAE by less than $0.005$, and the
cross-validated bandwidth matches the best fixed choice (Appendix~\ref{app:sens}).

\section{Discussion}
Comparing models does not require responses to identical queries; responses to similar
queries suffice. Evaluation data is rarely paired in practice: each model answers the
queries its evaluators chose, and methods that require a shared block of identical
queries do not apply. \PKPS removes this requirement by weighting response comparisons
with a query kernel, recovering paired DKPS in the $\sigma\!\to\!0$ limit. Our central
finding is that benchmark prediction remains possible under unpaired evaluation: as
$\rho\!\to\!0$, DKPS and IRT degrade sharply while \PKPS does not. This yields two
applications from a single embedding, denoising cheap evaluations and predicting
never-run cells, with the ensemble performing at least as well as either channel alone.

\paragraph{Limitations and future work.} \PKPS embeds every observed response and query
(embedder effects not ablated); the block-diagonal instantiation disables cross-domain
bridging (a shared response space is untested); the affinity is quadratic in queries per
model pair.
Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} is existential (no rate in $n$), and the DKPS separation
(Theorem~\ref{thm:sep}) covers fully unpaired designs; a rate in the pairing fraction
for $0<\rho<1$ is open. Finally, per-cell wins over score-only baselines are modest on dense
low-rank matrices and decisive in the sparse, noisy, unpaired regime that motivates the
problem.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}
All methods are released as a documented Python package (the \PKPS estimator, matrix
completion, and IRT baselines), with the data-preparation, experiment, and figure scripts
for both the synthetic study and the HELM suite; every experiment is seeded, all real-data
numbers are means over $16$ seeds (synthetic: $50$), and hyperparameters are selected by the
leak-free cross-validation described in Section~\ref{sec:method} (leakage is checked by an
automated test that corrupts one model's held-out scores and verifies its predictions are
unchanged). Appendix~\ref{app:details} lists the suite composition, embedding models, and
all hyperparameter settings.

\paragraph{\PKPS.} RBF query kernel with bandwidth selected per run by leave-one-model-out
$k$-NN ($k{=}8$) on the observed scores over the grid
$\sigma\in\{0.03,0.1,0.3,1,3\}\times\mathrm{median}$ pairwise query distance, plus the
near-delta (DKPS) limit; linear response kernel on real data, RBF on synthetic. Response
blocks are capped at $48$ PCA components per dataset and the shared query space at $64$;
each (model, query) pair uses one sampled response ($r{=}1$). Models are embedded by
classical MDS with $d{=}8$ throughout, following \citet{dkps_qeff} and fixed a priori
($d$ = latent dimension on synthetic). Appendix~\ref{app:sens} shows the results are
insensitive to $d$ over $4$--$24$, and that the cross-validated bandwidth tracks the best
fixed choice.

\paragraph{Prediction head.} All embedding methods share one head: logit-transformed
scores, per-task standardization to unit variance, a two-way (model $+$ task) additive
bias, and a distance-weighted $k$-NN ($k{=}5$) regression of the residual on the model
embedding, fit \emph{leave-one-family-out}: all models from the target's developer are
excluded from its training set, so predictions never lean on near-duplicate siblings
\citep{dkps_qeff}. Held-out cells are predicted directly; observed cells leave their own
target out.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Low-rank matrix completion (LRMC) follows BenchPress \citep{benchpress}: logit
space, per-task standardization, two-way bias, rank-$2$ ALS with ridge $\lambda{=}0.1$,
averaged over $5$ random initializations; on observed cells it is cross-fit so a cell's
prediction never sees its own sample. IRT is a 1PL (Rasch) model on binary tasks: item
difficulties by maximum likelihood (L-BFGS) on the largest fully-observed
(model $\times$ item) block, per-model ability by bounded scalar MLE, clipped to
$[-4,4]$. The sample baseline is the cell's own $m$-query mean.

\paragraph{Ensemble.} On observed cells, the sample mean is shrunk toward the \PKPS
estimate with per-cell precision weight $e^2_{\PKPS}/(e^2_{\PKPS}+\hat p(1-\hat p)/m)$ on
the sample, where $e^2_{\PKPS}$ is the embedding's depth-weighted disagreement with the
observed samples of that model; on missing cells, \PKPS and LRMC are blended
with a weight fit by held-out cross-validation against observed scores. No quantity ever
uses the held-out full-eval scores.

\paragraph{Sweeps and reporting.}\begin{sloppypar} All real-data sweeps use $16$ seeds; curves and table
entries report the per-seed mean over tasks, then the mean ($\pm1$ SEM) over seeds. Query
efficiency: budgets $m\in\{1,2,4,8,16,32\}$ at full cohort and coverage; cohort
$n\in\{10,20,40,60,93\}$ (HELM) or $\{10,20,30,45\}$ (EEE) and task coverage
$p_{\mathrm{task}}\in\{0.2,0.35,0.5,0.7,0.9\}$, both at the few-query point $m{=}2$.
Completion: task coverage as above and query depth
$p_{\mathrm{query}}\in\{0.1,0.25,0.5,0.75,1.0\}$ with cohort lines $n\in\{10,\text{full}\}$;
task count $T\in\{4,8,12,18\}$ (HELM) or $\{4,8,12,16\}$ (EEE); the cohort sweep holds
$p_{\mathrm{task}}{=}p_{\mathrm{query}}{=}0.5$.\end{sloppypar}

\paragraph{Synthetic study.} $50$ seeds per configuration; latent dimension $5$, observed
embedding dimension $20$, score noise $0.1$, response noise $0.5$; both kernels RBF;
$k$-NN regression with $k{=}5$. Panel-specific settings are given in each figure title.

\paragraph{Compute.} All experiments run on a single multi-core CPU node; text embeddings
are precomputed once through the embedding API and cached. Embedding the EEE suite
($30{,}304$ pooled responses plus $7{,}665$ queries, ${\sim}11$M tokens) took ${\sim}13$
minutes and under \$$2$ of API credit; each $16$-seed sweep completes in minutes (EEE) to
hours (HELM) on the same node.

\section{Sensitivity analyses}
\label{app:sens}

Figure~\ref{fig:sens} probes the two representation hyperparameters on the
query-efficiency experiment ($16$ seeds throughout). \textbf{MDS dimension (a, b).}
Repeating the budget sweep at $d\in\{4,8,24\}$ moves \PKPS's MAE by less than
$0.005$ at every budget on both suites (at the smallest budgets, smaller $d$ is very
slightly better---fewer coordinates to estimate from one noisy query per cell); the
paper's choice ($d{=}8$, following \citealp{dkps_qeff}) is never the worst point at any
budget. \textbf{Query bandwidth (c).} Fixing the
bandwidth at each grid multiplier instead of cross-validating traces the expected curve:
near-delta multipliers and the exact-matching $\delta$ limit are substantially worse
(unpaired pools leave them little to compare), broad multipliers are best, and the
leak-free cross-validated choice matches the best fixed multiplier on both suites
($0.126$ vs.\ $0.125$ on HELM, $0.069$ vs.\ $0.068$ on EEE)---the one hyperparameter
that matters is the one the method selects automatically. Sensitivity to the number of
reference models is part of the main experiments (the cohort sweeps of

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}}
\label{app:proof}

\paragraph{Population quantities.} Queries for model $a$ are drawn i.i.d.\ from a design
distribution over embedded queries; write $z=(u,x_a(u))$ for an embedded query--response
pair (response noise included) and $P_a$ for its law. By Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} the kernels are bounded:
$k_Q\in[0,1]$ after normalization (RBF) and $|k_R|\le B_R$. Define the population affinity
\[
A^*_{ab}\;=\;\frac{\mathbb{E}\,[k_Q(u,u')\,k_R(x_a(u),x_b(u'))]}{\mathbb{E}\,[k_Q(u,u')]},
\qquad z\sim P_a,\; z'\sim P_b \text{ independent},
\]
population squared distances $D^{*2}(a,b)=A^*_{aa}+A^*_{bb}-2A^*_{ab}$, the centered Gram
matrix $B^*=-\tfrac12 J D^{*2} J$ with $J=I-\tfrac1n\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$, and
population perspectives $\Psi^*\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ its rank-$d$ spectral embedding,
which exists with $\lambda_d(B^*)>0$ by Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} (cf.\ Definition~\ref{def:pop}).

\begin{lemma}[Affinity concentration]
\label{lem:conc}
Under Assumption~\ref{ass:reg}, for models $a,b$ with $\min(|Q_a|,|Q_b|)\ge m$ and any
$\delta\in(0,1)$: with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\[
|A_{ab}-A^*_{ab}|\;\le\;\frac{2(1+B_R)}{\kappa^2}\,t_m(\delta),
\qquad t_m(\delta)=\bar B\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2m}},\quad \bar B=\max(2B_R,1),
\]
provided $t_m(\delta)\le\kappa/2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
\begin{sloppypar}
The numerator $N=\frac{1}{|Q_a||Q_b|}\sum_{j,l}k_Q(u_j,u'_l)\,k_R(x_{aj},x_{bl})$ and
denominator $Z=\frac{1}{|Q_a||Q_b|}\sum_{j,l}k_Q(u_j,u'_l)$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:A} are
two-sample $U$-statistics of order $(1,1)$ in the i.i.d.\ pairs $z_j\sim P_a$,
$z'_l\sim P_b$, with kernels of range at most $2B_R$ and $1$ respectively (response noise
enters only through the bounded kernel argument, so the number of responses per query may
stay fixed). By Hoeffding's inequality for two-sample $U$-statistics
\citep[Section~5]{hoeffding1963}, each deviates from its mean by more than its range times
$\sqrt{\log(4/\delta)/2m}$ with probability at most $\delta/2$; since $\bar B$ dominates
both ranges, a union bound gives $|N-N^*|\le t_m(\delta)$ and $|Z-Z^*|\le t_m(\delta)$
jointly with probability $\ge1-\delta$. On this event, since $Z^*\ge\kappa$ by Assumption~\ref{ass:reg} and $t_m(\delta)\le\kappa/2$,
we have $Z\ge\kappa/2$, and
\[
|A_{ab}-A^*_{ab}|=\frac{|NZ^*-N^*Z|}{ZZ^*}
\;\le\;\frac{|N-N^*|}{Z}+\frac{|N^*|\,|Z-Z^*|}{ZZ^*}
\;\le\;\frac{2\,t_m(\delta)}{\kappa}+\frac{2B_R\,t_m(\delta)}{\kappa^2}
\;\le\;\frac{2(1+B_R)}{\kappa^2}\,t_m(\delta),
\]
using $|N^*|\le B_R$ and $\kappa\le1$.
\end{sloppypar}
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Perspective perturbation]
\label{lem:pert}
Let $\varepsilon_\infty=\max_{a,b}|A_{ab}-A^*_{ab}|$. There is an orthogonal
$W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ such that
\[
\max_i\;\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\;\le\;
\frac{25\sqrt{d}\;n\,\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}\,.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Entrywise, $|D^2-D^{*2}|\le4\varepsilon_\infty$, and double centering inflates an
entrywise bound by at most a factor of $4$, so $E=B-B^*$ satisfies
$\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le\|E\|_F\le8\,n\,\varepsilon_\infty$. Let $B_d$ be the top-$d$
truncation of $B$ that classical MDS embeds ($B_d=\hat\Psi\hat\Psi^\top$, positive
semidefinite). By Weyl's inequality every eigenvalue of $B$ beyond the $d$-th, and every
negative eigenvalue, has magnitude at most $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ (since
$\lambda_{d+1}(B^*)=0$ and $B^*\succeq0$), so
$\|B_d-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$. The matrix $B_d-B^*$ has rank at most
$2d$, hence
\[
\|B_d-B^*\|_F\;\le\;\sqrt{2d}\,\|B_d-B^*\|_{\mathrm{op}}
\;\le\;\sqrt{2d}\,\big(\|B_d-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}\big)
\;\le\;2\sqrt{2d}\,\|E\|_F .
\]
Both $B_d=\hat\Psi\hat\Psi^\top$ and $B^*=\Psi^*\Psi^{*\top}$ are positive semidefinite of
rank at most $d$, so by the Procrustes alignment lemma of
\citet[Lemma~5.4]{tu2016procrustes},
\[
\min_{W\in O(d)}\;\|\hat\Psi-\Psi^*W\|_F^2
\;\le\;\frac{\|B_d-B^*\|_F^2}{2(\sqrt2-1)\,\lambda_d(B^*)}\,.
\]
Combining, $\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le\|\hat\Psi-\Psi^*W\|_F\le
\frac{2\sqrt{2d}}{\sqrt{2(\sqrt2-1)}}\cdot\frac{8n\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}
\le\frac{25\sqrt d\,n\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}$. Nearest-neighbor
distances are invariant to $W$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}.} Take scores in $[0,1]$ without loss of
generality (Assumption~\ref{ass:score}). Fix $\epsilon>0$ and set targets $c\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}/(8\gamma)$ for
the estimation error and $\epsilon'\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}/(2\gamma)$ for the population
nearest-neighbor distance. By Assumption~\ref{ass:support} there is $n_0(\epsilon')$ such that for $n\ge n_0$, a
reference model within population distance $\epsilon'$ of the target exists except with
probability at most $\epsilon/8$. Fix such an $n$. Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:conc} with
confidence $\delta=\epsilon/(8n^2)$ to every pair, a union bound and
Lemma~\ref{lem:pert} give $\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le c$ except with probability at
most $\epsilon/8$, once
\[
m\;\ge\;C'\,\frac{d\,(1+B_R)^2\,\bar B^2\,n^2\log(n/\epsilon)}{\kappa^4\,\lambda_d\,c^2}
\;=\;\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\kappa,1/\epsilon),
\]
for an absolute constant $C'$ (this choice also satisfies the proviso
$t_m\le\kappa/2$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:conc}), and does not involve the pairing fraction
$\rho$. On the intersection of the two
events, for a target model $f$ with nearest estimated reference neighbor $f^*$
(perspectives $\psi^*,\hat\psi^*$; the target's $\psi,\hat\psi$),
\[
|\hat y(f)-y(f)|\;\le\;\gamma\,\|\psi^*-\psi\|\;\le\;
\gamma\big(\|\hat\psi^*-W\psi^*\|+\|\hat\psi^*-\hat\psi\|+\|\hat\psi-W\psi\|\big)
\;\le\;\gamma\,(2c+\hat\delta),
\]
using Assumption~\ref{ass:score} and orthogonal invariance. The estimated nearest-neighbor distance
$\hat\delta$ is at most the estimated distance to the $\epsilon'$-close reference, itself
at most $\epsilon'+2c$ by the same alignment argument, so
$|\hat y-y|\le\gamma(4c+\epsilon')\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}$. Squaring, and adding the two
failure events (squared errors are bounded by $1$),
$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat y)\le\epsilon/2+\epsilon/4\le\epsilon$. \hfill$\qed$

\paragraph{Relative to the sample score.} The sample score at budget $m$ is the mean of
$m$ i.i.d.\ per-query scores and uses no reference models, so its MSE is $\sigma_q^2/m$
for every $n$, where $\sigma_q^2$ is the per-query score variance ($\le1/4$ for scores
in $[0,1]$). For any $\epsilon<\sigma_q^2/m$ at the budget of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff},
the theorem's estimator satisfies $\mathrm{MSE}\le\epsilon<\sigma_q^2/m$ for all
sufficiently large $n$: query efficiency relative to the sample score in the sense of
Definition~\ref{def:qeff}.

\paragraph{Contrast with paired DKPS.} With $k_Q=\delta$, only identically-shared query
pairs enter the double sum, so the $U$-statistic runs over the $\rho M_{ij}$ shared queries
and the rate in Lemma~\ref{lem:conc} becomes $O_P\big((\rho M_{ij})^{-1/2}\big)$: DKPS's
guarantee requires $\rho M_{ij}\to\infty$ and vacates in the unpaired limit $\rho\to0$,
where its distance is undefined (Section~\ref{sec:method}).

\paragraph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:sep}.} Nonatomic design distributions give
$\Pr(q_j=q'_l)=0$ for every pair of independently drawn queries, so with probability one
$Q_a\cap Q_b=\emptyset$ for every pair of models and every identity-matched sum in the
paired affinity is empty. Any measurable completion $\hat y$ of a paired-DKPS estimator
is therefore a function of the reference data alone, and the target model is drawn
independently of the references, so $y\perp\hat y$ and
$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat y)=\mathbb{E}[(y-\hat y)^2]
=v+\mathbb{E}[(\mathbb{E}y-\hat y)^2]\ge v$ at every $(n,m)$. By
Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}, for any $\epsilon<v$ there exist $(n,m)$ at which \PKPS attains
$\mathrm{MSE}\le\epsilon<v$ with high probability. In the sense of
Definition~\ref{def:qeff}, \PKPS is query-efficient relative to paired DKPS: the
comparator's error is bounded away from zero at every budget, and \PKPS falls below it.
\hfill$\qed$
