\title{Product Kernel Perspective Spaces:\\ Predicting Benchmark Scores from Sparse, Unpaired Model Evaluations}
\author{Anonymous}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Evaluating language models is expensive, so benchmark coverage is sparse and noisy: each
model is run on only some tasks, only some queries within a task, and each score is a noisy
average over those queries. Methods that complete the (model $\times$ task) score
matrix---matrix completion, item-response theory---use only each cell's \emph{aggregate}
score. We use \emph{item-level} information instead: a cell's individual responses, not just
its score. We introduce the \textbf{Product Kernel Perspective Space (\PKPS)}, which embeds
each model from its responses through a product kernel $k_Q\cdot k_R$ that compares responses
to \emph{similar} queries, not only identical ones; it recovers the Data Kernel Perspective
Space as the bandwidth $\sigma\!\to\!0$ and stays defined when models answer different
queries. Ensembling this embedding with the available score signal---a cell's own sample mean
where measured, matrix completion where not---makes efficient benchmarking and benchmark
completion two ends of one problem. On a heterogeneous $18$-task, $93$-model suite, the
ensemble matches or beats the best score-only predictor across task coverage, queries per
cell, and cohort size, by a margin that grows as evaluation gets sparser and noisier; the
embedding survives unpaired queries where DKPS and IRT collapse, and rescues
small cohorts where low-rank completion fails for lack of rows.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Comprehensive evaluation of language models is a growing bottleneck: leaderboards
track hundreds of models across dozens of benchmarks, yet no model is run on
everything---a recent compilation of $84$ frontier models on $133$ benchmarks finds
only $23\%$ of the score matrix filled \citep{benchpress}. The resulting
(model $\times$ task) score matrix is \emph{doubly sparse}. \emph{Across tasks}, a
model is evaluated on a subset of benchmarks (missing tasks). \emph{Within a task},
evaluation may use only a subset of queries to save cost (missing queries).
Predicting the missing entries---a model's score on a task it has not been (fully)
run on---would let practitioners reuse cached evaluations instead of paying to re-run
them \citep{polo2024tinybenchmarks, vivek2024anchor, benchpress}.

The Data Kernel Perspective Space (DKPS) \citep{dkps_qeff} is a black-box approach:
it embeds each model from its query responses, places models in a low-dimensional
Euclidean space via classical multidimensional scaling, and regresses benchmark
scores in that space. DKPS is query-efficient and competitive with item-response
theory. However, it has a structural limitation: the distance between two models is the
average squared difference of their responses \emph{to the same queries}, so DKPS
requires a \emph{paired} design. Real evaluation data is routinely unpaired: arena-style
platforms score each model on whatever conversations its users happen to have
\citep{chiang2024chatbot}; contamination-driven benchmarks refresh their items, so models
evaluated in different months answer different questions \citep{white2024livebench}; and
cost-driven subsampling \citep{perlitz2024efficient} gives each evaluation run its own
item subset. In all of these, DKPS has nothing to compare and the information in the
unpaired responses is thrown away.

We introduce the \textbf{Product Kernel Perspective Space (\PKPS)}. The key change
is to compare responses to \emph{similar} queries, not only identical ones, by
weighting each response-pair comparison with a query kernel. This (i) lets every
unpaired response contribute, and (ii) contains DKPS as the special case where the
query kernel is the identity. The broader thesis is that \PKPS supplies an
\emph{item-level embedding} signal that score-completion baselines ignore, and that
combining it with the available \emph{item-level score} signal predicts benchmark
performance more efficiently than either alone. Our contributions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Methodological and theoretical.} We extend DKPS to the unpaired regime with a
product-kernel affinity $k_Q\cdot k_R$ that compares responses to similar queries
(Section~\ref{sec:method}). We show the construction is a strict generalization: it contains
paired DKPS as the special case $k_Q=\delta$ and recovers it as the bandwidth
$\sigma\!\to\!0$, stays defined when models answer disjoint queries (where paired DKPS is
undefined), and interpolates between the two with a single bandwidth, which we select per run
by a leak-free cross-validation. Extending the proof technique of \citet{dkps_qeff}, we give a
query-efficiency guarantee whose query requirement is independent of the pairing fraction
(Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}), state the strict gap over paired DKPS as a conjecture, and treat the
embedding as one term of an embedding-$\oplus$-score ensemble.
\item \textbf{Empirical.} We demonstrate the method's utility and robustness to missing
responses on the two ends of one estimation problem---query-efficient evaluation ($m{>}0$
queries per cell) and matrix completion ($m{=}0$)---over a heterogeneous $18$-task, $93$-model
suite spanning math, translation, medical QA, and legal classification
(Sections~\ref{sec:synthetic}--\ref{sec:real}). The embedding survives unpaired queries where
DKPS and IRT collapse and recovers the small-cohort regime where low-rank completion fails for
lack of rows; the ensemble matches or beats the best score-only predictor across task
coverage, queries per cell, and cohort size, by a margin that grows as evaluation gets
sparser, noisier, or smaller-cohort.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Low-dimensional representations of models.} Our method contributes to a literature
on low-dimensional representations of models. Some methods compare models through their
internals: activations on shared inputs \citep{duderstadt2023comparing, huh2024platonic,
horwitz2025atlas} or weights \citep{chen2025weights}. Evaluation, however, is black-box: it sees
responses, not internals. The Data Kernel Perspective Space (DKPS) embeds models from their
responses into a low-dimensional Euclidean space via multidimensional scaling
\citep{torgerson1952mds, helm2024tracking}. The representations are consistent and concentrate
\citep{acharyya2024consistent, acharyya2025concentration}, support statistical inference
\citep{helm2025inference}, and are query-efficient \citep{dkps_qeff}. DKPS averages over
responses to \emph{shared} queries, so it requires a paired design. \PKPS keeps the
response-level representation but replaces identity matching with a product kernel, so models
that answer different queries remain comparable.

\paragraph{Benchmark prediction.} A second line predicts unobserved (model, task) scores so that
not every evaluation must be run; efficient benchmarking and matrix completion are its two ends.
Efficient benchmarking scores a small item subset and extrapolates. Item-response theory selects
items by difficulty and discrimination \citep{rasch1960, polo2024tinybenchmarks, metabench};
anchor points cluster items by cross-model agreement \citep{vivek2024anchor}; cognitive-scale
embeddings \citep{bean2025scales} and reinforcement learning \citep{li2024active} select items
adaptively; and design analyses optimize the cost--reliability tradeoff
\citep{perlitz2024efficient}. Closer to us, DKPS predicts a held-out score from cached responses
\citep{dkps_qeff}, datamodels predict outputs from inputs \citep{ilyas2022datamodels}, and
lifelong benchmarks reuse past evaluations \citep{prabhu2024lifelong}. Matrix completion instead
predicts cells that were never run, exploiting low rank across the score matrix
\citep{candes2009exact, mazumder2010softimpute, koren2009matrix}; BenchPress finds it is
approximately rank-two and completes it in logit space \citep{benchpress}. These methods use only
each cell's \emph{aggregate} score, not its responses. \PKPS unifies the two ends---a cell with
$m\ge0$ queries spans efficient benchmarking ($m{>}0$) and completion ($m{=}0$)---and adds an
item-level embedding signal that we ensemble with matrix completion.

\section{The Product Kernel Perspective Space}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Models and observations.} A \emph{model} is a random mapping
$f:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathcal{X}$ from a query space $\mathcal{Q}$ to a response space
$\mathcal{X}$; querying $f$ at $q$ returns a response drawn from the distribution $f(q)$.
We observe $n$ models $f_1,\dots,f_n$, each run on its own \emph{query set}
$Q_i\subseteq\mathcal{Q}$, giving a tuple $\mathbf{Q}=(Q_1,\dots,Q_n)$ that may differ
across models or be disjoint; the paired design $Q_1=\dots=Q_n$ is the special case. Two
fixed, distinct embedding functions map raw objects to vectors: a \emph{response}
embedding $\mathrm{embed}_R:\mathcal{X}\to\mathbb{R}^{p}$ and a \emph{query} embedding
$\mathrm{embed}_Q:\mathcal{Q}\to\mathbb{R}^{p'}$ (e.g.\ different text-embedding models).
For $q_j\in Q_i$ we average the embeddings of the $r$ sampled responses,
$x_{ij}=\tfrac1r\sum_{k=1}^{r}\mathrm{embed}_R\!\big(f_i(q_j)^{(k)}\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{p}$,
and write $u_j=\mathrm{embed}_Q(q_j)$ for the embedded query. The goal is to predict each
model's true score on each task, $y_{it}\in\mathcal{Y}$---including tasks the model was run
on with few queries (denoising) and tasks it was never run on (completion); we write $y_i$
when the benchmark has a single score.

\paragraph{Perspective space.} Each method summarizes the $n$ models in an $n\times n$
affinity matrix $A$ (defined below). The squared distance between two models is
$D^2(a,b)=A_{aa}+A_{bb}-2A_{ab}$ (clamped at $0$), and their $d$-dimensional
\emph{representations} are the classical-multidimensional-scaling minimizer
\begin{equation}
(\hat\psi_1,\dots,\hat\psi_n)=\operatorname*{arg\,min}_{z_1,\dots,z_n\in\mathbb{R}^d}\
\sum_{i,k=1}^{n}\big(\|z_i-z_k\|-D(i,k)\big)^2,
\label{eq:mds}
\end{equation}
so model $i$ is the point $\hat\psi_i\in\mathbb{R}^d$, its \emph{perspective}. Paired DKPS
and \PKPS differ only in the affinity $A$.

\paragraph{Paired DKPS.} With all $n$ models answering the same $m$ queries
($Q_1=\dots=Q_n$), DKPS uses the identity-matched affinity
$A_{ab}=\tfrac{1}{m}\sum_j k_R(x_{aj},x_{bj})$: response $j$ of model $a$ is paired only
with response $j$ of model $b$, so the design must be paired.

\paragraph{Product kernel.} \PKPS replaces identity matching with a query kernel $k_Q$:
\begin{equation}
A_{ab} = \frac{\sum_{j\in Q_a}\sum_{l\in Q_b} k_Q(q_j,q_l)\, k_R(x_{aj},x_{bl})}
              {\sum_{j\in Q_a}\sum_{l\in Q_b} k_Q(q_j,q_l)},
\label{eq:A}
\end{equation}
where $Q_a$ is the query set of model $a$. Each term is normalized by its own query-kernel
mass, so models that answered different numbers of (or entirely different) queries are
still comparable; responses to \emph{similar} queries---even across tasks---contribute and
unpaired evaluations are used (Figure~\ref{fig:concept}). Paired DKPS is the special case
$k_Q=\delta$, where only $j=l$ survives.

\paragraph{Kernels.} The response kernel $k_R$ compares embedded responses, the query
kernel $k_Q$ compares embedded queries. Paired DKPS uses a linear response kernel and the
identity query kernel $k_Q=\delta$. We use an RBF query kernel over the embedded queries,
$k_Q(q_j,q_l)=\exp(-\|u_j-u_l\|^2/2\sigma^2)$, with bandwidth $\sigma$ chosen per run
(below); $k_R$ is RBF in the synthetic study and linear on real data, where linearity makes
the kernel factorize across response domains (Section~\ref{sec:real}). Because
Eq.~\ref{eq:A} normalizes each pair by its own kernel mass, $A$ is a ratio of kernel sums
and need not be positive semidefinite; as is standard for such dissimilarities, classical
MDS embeds the doubly-centered matrix using its top-$d$ nonnegative eigenvalues. Computing
$A_{ab}$ costs $|Q_a||Q_b|$ kernel terms per model pair, and one pass over the
response-kernel terms serves every query bandwidth on the grid.

\subsection{Theoretical properties of the \PKPS}
\PKPS is a strict generalization of DKPS.
\begin{proposition}[Strict generalization]
\label{prop:gen}
(i)~\emph{DKPS limit.} With $k_Q=\delta$, Eq.~\ref{eq:A} reduces to paired DKPS over the
shared queries, $A_{ab}=\tfrac{1}{|Q_a\cap Q_b|}\sum_{q\in Q_a\cap Q_b}k_R(x_{aq},x_{bq})$;
if the query embeddings are distinct, the RBF query kernel converges to $\delta$ as
$\sigma\!\to\!0$, so DKPS is also the small-bandwidth limit.
(ii)~\emph{Unpaired comparability.} For any strictly positive $k_Q$ (e.g.\ RBF), $A_{ab}$
is defined for every pair of nonempty query sets, including disjoint ones, whereas paired
DKPS is undefined when $Q_a\cap Q_b=\emptyset$.
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof}
(i) Take $k_Q(q_j,q_l)=\mathbf{1}[q_j=q_l]$. Since the double sum ranges over $j\in Q_a$,
$l\in Q_b$, a term survives only if the two queries are equal \emph{and} that query lies in
both sets, $\mathbf{1}[q_j=q_l]\,\mathbf{1}[q_j\in Q_a]\,\mathbf{1}[q_l\in Q_b]
=\mathbf{1}[q_j=q_l\in Q_a\cap Q_b]$; the normalizer counts the same terms, giving the
stated average (the same-$m$-queries case recovers $\tfrac1m\sum_j k_R(x_{aj},x_{bj})$).
For the limit, $\exp(-\|u_j-u_l\|^2/2\sigma^2)\to\mathbf{1}[u_j=u_l]$ pointwise as
$\sigma\to0$. (ii) With $k_Q>0$ the normalizer $\sum_{jl}k_Q(q_j,q_l)$ is positive for any
nonempty $Q_a,Q_b$, so the ratio in Eq.~\ref{eq:A} is well defined.
\end{proof}
Between the two limits, increasing $\sigma$ smoothly trades exact query matching for
cross-query bridging, so a single scalar controls how much unpaired evidence the
perspective uses.

\subsection{Inference in the \PKPS}
Inference is a two-step statistical problem, exactly as in the DKPS \citep{dkps_qeff}:
embed each model into the perspective space by the scaling of Eq.~\ref{eq:mds}, then
regress its benchmark score on the resulting coordinate. Concretely, on a set of reference
models with known scores $\{(f_i,y_i)\}$ we fit a decision function
$\hat h:\mathbb{R}^d\to\mathcal{Y}$ on the pairs $\{(\hat\psi_i,y_i)\}$ and predict any
model's score as $\hat y(f)=\hat h(\hat\psi(f))$. The only free choices are the
query-kernel bandwidth $\sigma$ and the regressor $\hat h$, which we describe next; we then
add the score channel that turns the embedding into an ensemble.

\paragraph{Choosing the bandwidth.} The optimal $\sigma$ depends on how paired the
data is: small when query overlap is high (recover exact matching), large when
overlap is low (bridge across queries). We set it per run by cross-validation over a
median-scaled grid $\sigma\in\{0.03,\dots,3\}\cdot\mathrm{med}$, where $\mathrm{med}$
is the median pairwise query distance, selecting the $\sigma$ whose perspective best predicts
the \emph{observed} scores under leave-one-model-out $k$-NN. The criterion never touches the
held-out scores we report, so the choice is not circular with the evaluation; and because the
grid spans down to near-delta, cross-validation \emph{recovers DKPS} when query overlap is
high and bridges only when it must. One response-kernel pass produces the entire
$\sigma$-grid, so tuning adds negligible cost. We use a single regressor (distance-weighted $k$-NN)
and a logit-space, bias-decomposed head throughout.

\paragraph{Estimation pipeline.} For each replicate we (i) reduce response and query
embeddings by PCA on the \emph{observed} rows only (dimension by the second Zhu--Ghodsi
elbow); (ii) form $D$ from Eq.~\ref{eq:A}; (iii) embed models by classical MDS; (iv)
map the embedding to scores with the \emph{same} head as the matrix-completion baseline
up to the factor model---logit transform, per-task standardization to unit variance,
and a two-way (model $+$ task) additive bias---then a $k$-NN regression of the residual
on the embedding (replacing completion's low-rank factor), predicting every cell
(held-out cells directly, observed cells leave-one-out so the estimate never sees its
own target). Matching the per-task standardization is important: without it the model
offset is dominated by the highest-logit-variance tasks, which systematically biases the
embedding on heterogeneous, multi-scale suites.

\paragraph{The embedding-$\oplus$-score ensemble.} For each cell we combine the
embedding with whichever score signal carries independent information. On a
\emph{missing} cell, that is matrix completion of the (noisy) score matrix
\citep{benchpress}; we blend it with \PKPS using a weight fit by held-out
cross-validation against the observed scores. On an \emph{observed} cell, the cell's
own sample mean is the score signal (matrix completion coincides with it there); we
shrink it toward the \PKPS estimate by a precision weight $\sigma_q^2/(\sigma_q^2+
e_{\PKPS}^2)$---the sample's noise variance over its total disagreement with \PKPS---so
the own sample dominates as queries grow and the embedding dominates as noise grows.
Both quantities are read off the observed queries---the sample noise variance estimated as
$\hat p(1-\hat p)/m$ and the prior error from its depth-weighted disagreement with the
sample---so the weight never touches the held-out full scores and the score baselines see
exactly the same information.

\subsection{Query efficiency}
\label{sec:qeff}
\PKPS is designed to reach a target prediction accuracy from fewer evaluated queries than
paired DKPS: by bridging responses to similar queries it uses evidence that DKPS, limited
to shared queries, discards. The query-efficiency analysis of \citet{dkps_qeff} extends to
\PKPS. Assume (A1) the benchmark score is bounded and $\gamma$-Lipschitz on the population
perspective space; (A2) the model distribution places mass on every neighborhood, so a near
reference neighbor exists; (A3) kernels and embedded responses are bounded; (A4) the
\emph{cross-model query-kernel mass} is bounded below,
$\kappa=\inf_{a,b}\,\mathbb{E}\,[k_Q(u,u')]>0$ for queries drawn from any two models'
designs; and (A5) the population squared-distance matrix embeds in $\mathbb{R}^d$, with a
spectral gap $\lambda_d>0$ in its centered Gram matrix. (A4) is the population analogue of
the overlap $\rho$: for an RBF $k_Q$ it holds whenever the query \emph{distributions}
overlap in embedding space, even if no individual query is shared.
\begin{theorem}[Query efficiency of \PKPS]
\label{thm:qeff}
Under (A1)--(A5), for any $\epsilon>0$ there exist $(n,m)$, with $m$ polynomial in
$(1/\epsilon,1/\kappa,n)$ and independent of the pairing fraction $\rho$, such that
nearest-neighbor regression in the \PKPS attains mean squared error at most $\epsilon$
with high probability.
\end{theorem}
The proof (Appendix~\ref{app:proof}) follows the DKPS argument: a
triangle-inequality decomposition of the regression error into estimation error,
controlled by concentration of the estimated perspectives, and approximation error,
controlled by (A1)--(A2). The one new ingredient is a concentration lemma for the
self-normalized affinity of Eq.~\ref{eq:A}, a ratio of two-sample $U$-statistics over
$|Q_a||Q_b|$ query pairs whose effective sample size is governed by $\kappa$. Applied to
paired DKPS ($k_Q=\delta$), the same argument retains only the $\rho M_{ij}$ shared pairs,
so DKPS's guarantee degrades as $\rho\to0$ while Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} does not. The
strict separation---a matching lower bound for DKPS---we leave as a conjecture;
Section~\ref{sec:synthetic} is its empirical counterpart.
\begin{conjecture}[Strict gap]
\label{conj:qeff}
Under (A1)--(A4), the number of queries per model that \PKPS needs to reach error
$\epsilon$ is at most that of paired DKPS, and the gap increases as $\rho\to0$; the two
are closest in the fully paired limit $\rho=1$.
\end{conjecture}

\section{Experiments}
A real evaluation matrix is governed by four observation levers. The defining one is
(i) \emph{pairing}---the fraction $\rho$ of each cell's queries shared across models, from
fully paired ($\rho{=}1$) to fully unpaired ($\rho{=}0$); it is the axis DKPS cannot handle,
and we sweep it directly in the synthetic study (Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}). The other three
set how sparse and noisy the observations are: (ii) \emph{task coverage}---the fraction of
(model, task) cells run at all; (iii) \emph{queries per cell}---how many queries a run uses,
which sets the per-cell measurement \emph{noise}; and (iv) \emph{cohort size}---the number of
models. On real data (Section~\ref{sec:real}) we turn to the two regimes practitioners actually
face, both inherently unpaired since each model is run on its own queries: the
all-tasks-covered, few-query limit is efficient benchmarking (denoising) and the zero-query
limit is benchmark completion---two applications of the same embedding.

\subsection{Synthetic data}
\label{sec:synthetic}
We generate $n$ models with latent ability vectors $v_i$ and $T$ tasks with latent
vectors $t_k$, scores $v_i\cdot t_k+\epsilon$, and queries drawn near each task center.
Each model answers $M_{ij}$ queries per task, of which $m_{ii'}$ are shared with another
model (overlap $\rho=m_{ii'}/M_{ij}$); a task is observed with probability $p_{\mathrm{task}}$
and a query with probability $p_{\mathrm{query}}$. We predict held-out (model, task)
scores and compare DKPS (identity query kernel) against \PKPS (RBF), with the score-only
sample baseline (predict a held-out cell by its task's observed mean) as a floor
(Figure~\ref{fig:synthetic}).

\PKPS---selecting its query bandwidth by the same leak-free cross-validation we use on real
data---beats DKPS across \emph{every} lever, and both beat the sample floor: bridging
responses to similar queries (across tasks as well as within) supplies model-similarity signal
that exact-match DKPS cannot reach, so the margin is wide even where queries are mostly paired
(panels a--c). The gap is largest where pairing is scarce: as query coverage
$p_{\mathrm{query}}$ drops (d) DKPS degrades while \PKPS stays flat, and sweeping the overlap
$\rho$ directly (e), DKPS collapses toward the sample floor as $\rho\!\to\!0$ while \PKPS is
essentially flat---it does not rely on pairing.

Panel (f) is the \emph{denoising} form of the same problem: each cell is now \emph{observed}
with $M_{ij}$ noisy queries (sample noise $\propto1/\sqrt{M_{ij}}$) and we predict its true
score. The raw sample mean (gray) falls like $1/\sqrt{M_{ij}}$; smoothing those noisy scores
over the perspective denoises well below it while measurement noise dominates---\PKPS reaches
at a single query the accuracy the raw sample needs roughly five queries to match, and does
so whether queries are paired or not, while DKPS denoises only when paired (unpaired DKPS,
dashed, barely improves). Only once the budget is large is the raw sample precise enough to
overtake the embedding's residual bias. This is the empirical counterpart of
Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} and Conjecture~\ref{conj:qeff}.

\subsection{Real data}
\label{sec:real}
We evaluate on one realistic, heterogeneous benchmark, studying the two applications the
unpaired embedding enables: making evaluation more \emph{query-efficient} (denoising cheap,
few-query runs) and completing \emph{missing} cells. Both are inherently \emph{unpaired}---each
model is run on its own queries, the real-data counterpart of the $\rho\!\to\!0$ limit swept in
the synthetic study: DKPS's identity matching has little to align and IRT's shared-anchor fit
cannot be estimated, while \PKPS bridges similar queries. This is where the unpaired capability
pays off in practice. All real-data numbers are means over $16$ seeds, and \PKPS selects its
query bandwidth by cross-validation per run. The suite is
$18$ tasks across four datasets with very different response types---MATH ($7$ math
subjects), WMT-14 ($5$ translation directions), MedQA (medical multiple-choice), and
LegalBench ($5$ legal classification subsets)---on the $93$ models evaluated on all four.
MATH and WMT have rich free-text responses; MedQA and LegalBench responses are single
tokens for which text embeddings are degenerate, so we encode each dataset's responses
natively (Gemini embeddings vs.\ one-hot answers) in disjoint blocks. With a linear $k_R$
this makes cross-dataset response similarity exactly zero, and a single shared query
embedding with a within-domain bandwidth keeps $k_Q$ near zero across domains: the
product kernel factorizes by domain, never comparing a proof to a legal label.

\paragraph{Application 1: query efficiency.} Because the embedding captures response
\emph{style}, which is stable even when the per-cell sample mean is noisy, it denoises
cheap evaluations. Each model answers $m$ queries per cell, sampled independently---so this
is itself an unpaired regime: IRT, whose item difficulties can only be fit on the largest
block of coincidentally-shared items, is poorly identified; we predict the true full-eval
score (Figure~\ref{fig:qe}). At a small budget the embedding is far more accurate than the
raw mean: at $m{=}1$, \PKPS scores $0.136$ and the ensemble $0.133$ versus the sample's
$0.292$; \PKPS at one query roughly matches the sample at four. As the budget grows the
raw mean catches up and eventually overtakes the embedding's residual bias ($0.034$ vs.\
$0.088$ at $m{=}32$); the leak-free ensemble tracks the better channel to within a few
thousandths. The advantage is robust across cohort size and task coverage (panels b, c).
Per cell, the denoising win is broad but tail-driven: the ensemble beats the raw sample on
most cells, with the largest gains at the smallest budgets and on the rich-response datasets
(Figure~\ref{fig:qecond}).

\paragraph{Application 2: matrix completion.} The same embedding also predicts cells that
were \emph{never run}. We observe a fraction $p_{\mathrm{task}}$ of cells (each at depth
$p_{\mathrm{query}}$) and predict the missing ones, against a low-rank matrix-completion
baseline (the sample does not apply---a missing cell has no sample). The contrast is the
cohort: low-rank completion needs many rows, so when models are scarce it fails for lack
of them while the embedding, which needs none, remains accurate---at $n{=}10$, completion is $0.153$
versus \PKPS's $0.127$ (Figure~\ref{fig:completion}, dashed). At full cohort ($n{=}93$,

completion cannot reach. The per-cell win over completion is modest and tail-driven---completion is
strong on this low-rank suite---but stays positive in the mean across datasets, cohorts,

\section{Discussion}
Real leaderboards are assembled organically: models are run on whatever query subsets
their authors chose, so the responses that benchmark estimation must compare are
\emph{unpaired}. DKPS and item-response models need a shared block of identical queries
and collapse without it. \PKPS removes that requirement with a product kernel
$k_Q\!\cdot\!k_R$ that compares responses to \emph{similar} queries, recovering paired
DKPS exactly in its $\sigma\!\to\!0$ limit and scaling to a heterogeneous $18$-task suite
through a block-diagonal kernel. Our central finding is that benchmark estimation remains accurate under unpaired
evaluation: as the shared fraction $\rho\!\to\!0$, DKPS and IRT degrade sharply while \PKPS holds.
That capability yields two practical applications from the same embedding---it denoises
cheap, few-query evaluations (query efficiency) and predicts cells that were never run
(matrix completion), and ensembled with a cell's own sample where one exists it is never
the worse choice. None of this assumes pairing or exhaustive per-cell evaluation, which is
exactly what organically-assembled leaderboards cannot provide.

\paragraph{Limitations.} \PKPS requires embedding every response and query, so its cost and
quality inherit from the chosen text embedders, whose effect we do not ablate. Our
block-diagonal instantiation deliberately disables cross-domain bridging, so the method
cannot transfer signal between, say, math and legal responses; whether a shared response
space would help is open. Computing the affinity is quadratic in queries per model pair,
which is practical at benchmark scale but would need approximation for very large query
logs. Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff} is existential---(A2) yields no explicit rate in the number
of reference models---and the strict separation from paired DKPS remains a conjecture.
Finally, our real-data evidence comes from one (albeit
heterogeneous) $18$-task suite; the per-cell wins over strong score-only baselines are
modest on low-rank, densely-observed matrices and decisive only where evaluation is sparse,
noisy, unpaired, or small-cohort---which is, however, the regime that motivates the problem.

\subsubsection*{Reproducibility statement}
All methods are released as a documented Python package (the \PKPS estimator, matrix
completion, and IRT baselines), with the data-preparation, experiment, and figure scripts
for both the synthetic study and the HELM suite; every experiment is seeded, all real-data
numbers are means over $16$ seeds (synthetic: $50$), and hyperparameters are selected by the
leak-free cross-validation described in Section~\ref{sec:method} (leakage is checked by an
automated test that corrupts one model's held-out scores and verifies its predictions are
unchanged). Appendix~\ref{app:details} lists the suite composition, embedding models, and
all hyperparameter settings.

\section{Experimental details}
\label{app:details}

\paragraph{Suite.} $18$ tasks over four datasets from HELM \citep{helm}: MATH ($7$
subjects), WMT-14 ($5$ translation directions), MedQA, and LegalBench ($5$ subsets), on the
$93$ models evaluated on all four. MATH and WMT-14 responses are free text and are embedded
with \texttt{gemini-embedding-001} ($3072$-d); MedQA and LegalBench responses are
single-token answers, encoded one-hot. Queries share a single \texttt{gemini-embedding-001}
embedding space. Response and query embeddings are reduced by PCA fit on observed rows
only, with dimension chosen by the second Zhu--Ghodsi elbow.

\paragraph{\PKPS.} RBF query kernel with bandwidth selected per run by leave-one-model-out
$k$-NN ($k{=}8$) on the observed scores over the grid
$\sigma\in\{0.03,0.1,0.3,1,3\}\times\mathrm{median}$ pairwise query distance, plus the
near-delta (DKPS) limit; linear response kernel on real data, RBF on synthetic. Models are
embedded by classical MDS ($d{=}12$ real data; $d$ = latent dimension on synthetic).

\paragraph{Prediction head.} All embedding methods share one head: logit-transformed
scores, per-task standardization to unit variance, a two-way (model $+$ task) additive
bias, and a distance-weighted $k$-NN ($k{=}5$) regression of the residual on the model
embedding. Held-out cells are predicted directly; observed cells leave-one-out.

\paragraph{Baselines.} Matrix completion follows BenchPress \citep{benchpress}: logit
space, per-task standardization, two-way bias, rank-$2$ ALS with ridge $\lambda{=}0.1$,
averaged over $5$ random initializations; on observed cells it is cross-fit so a cell's
prediction never sees its own sample. IRT is a 1PL (Rasch) model on binary tasks: item
difficulties by maximum likelihood (L-BFGS) on the largest fully-observed
(model $\times$ item) block, per-model ability by bounded scalar MLE, clipped to
$[-4,4]$. The sample baseline is the cell's own $m$-query mean.

\paragraph{Ensemble.} On observed cells, the sample mean is shrunk toward the \PKPS
estimate with per-cell precision weight $e^2_{\PKPS}/(e^2_{\PKPS}+\hat p(1-\hat p)/m)$ on
the sample, where $e^2_{\PKPS}$ is the embedding's depth-weighted disagreement with the
observed samples of that model; on missing cells, \PKPS and matrix completion are blended
with a weight fit by held-out cross-validation against observed scores. No quantity ever
uses the held-out full-eval scores.

\paragraph{Synthetic study.} $50$ seeds per configuration; latent dimension $5$, observed
embedding dimension $20$, score noise $0.1$, response noise $0.5$; both kernels RBF;
$k$-NN regression with $k{=}5$. Panel-specific settings are given in each figure title.

\paragraph{Compute.} All experiments run on a single multi-core CPU node; text embeddings
are precomputed once through the embedding API and cached.

\section{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}}
\label{app:proof}

\paragraph{Population quantities.} Queries for model $a$ are drawn i.i.d.\ from a design
distribution over embedded queries; write $z=(u,x_a(u))$ for an embedded query--response
pair (response noise included) and $P_a$ for its law. By (A3) the kernels are bounded:
$k_Q\in[0,1]$ after normalization (RBF) and $|k_R|\le B_R$. Define the population affinity
\[
A^*_{ab}\;=\;\frac{\mathbb{E}\,[k_Q(u,u')\,k_R(x_a(u),x_b(u'))]}{\mathbb{E}\,[k_Q(u,u')]},
\qquad z\sim P_a,\; z'\sim P_b \text{ independent},
\]
population squared distances $D^{*2}(a,b)=A^*_{aa}+A^*_{bb}-2A^*_{ab}$, the centered Gram
matrix $B^*=-\tfrac12 J D^{*2} J$ with $J=I-\tfrac1n\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^\top$, and
population perspectives $\Psi^*\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times d}$ its rank-$d$ spectral embedding,
which exists with $\lambda_d(B^*)>0$ by (A5).

\begin{lemma}[Affinity concentration]
\label{lem:conc}
Under (A3)--(A4), for models $a,b$ with $\min(|Q_a|,|Q_b|)\ge m$ and any
$\delta\in(0,1)$: with probability at least $1-\delta$,
\[
|A_{ab}-A^*_{ab}|\;\le\;\frac{2(1+B_R)}{\kappa^2}\,t_m(\delta),
\qquad t_m(\delta)=\bar B\sqrt{\frac{\log(4/\delta)}{2m}},\quad \bar B=\max(2B_R,1),
\]
provided $t_m(\delta)\le\kappa/2$.
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
The numerator $N=\frac{1}{|Q_a||Q_b|}\sum_{j,l}k_Q(u_j,u'_l)\,k_R(x_{aj},x_{bl})$ and
denominator $Z=\frac{1}{|Q_a||Q_b|}\sum_{j,l}k_Q(u_j,u'_l)$ of Eq.~\ref{eq:A} are
two-sample $U$-statistics of order $(1,1)$ in the i.i.d.\ pairs $z_j\sim P_a$,
$z'_l\sim P_b$, with kernels of range at most $2B_R$ and $1$ respectively (response noise
enters only through the bounded kernel argument, so the number of responses per query may
stay fixed). By Hoeffding's inequality for two-sample $U$-statistics
\citep[Section~5]{hoeffding1963}, each deviates from its mean by more than its range times
$\sqrt{\log(4/\delta)/2m}$ with probability at most $\delta/2$; since $\bar B$ dominates
both ranges, a union bound gives $|N-N^*|\le t_m(\delta)$ and $|Z-Z^*|\le t_m(\delta)$
jointly with probability $\ge1-\delta$. On this event, since $Z^*\ge\kappa$ by (A4) and $t_m(\delta)\le\kappa/2$,
we have $Z\ge\kappa/2$, and
\[
|A_{ab}-A^*_{ab}|=\frac{|NZ^*-N^*Z|}{ZZ^*}
\;\le\;\frac{|N-N^*|}{Z}+\frac{|N^*|\,|Z-Z^*|}{ZZ^*}
\;\le\;\frac{2\,t_m(\delta)}{\kappa}+\frac{2B_R\,t_m(\delta)}{\kappa^2}
\;\le\;\frac{2(1+B_R)}{\kappa^2}\,t_m(\delta),
\]
using $|N^*|\le B_R$ and $\kappa\le1$.
\end{proof}

\begin{lemma}[Perspective perturbation]
\label{lem:pert}
Let $\varepsilon_\infty=\max_{a,b}|A_{ab}-A^*_{ab}|$. There is an orthogonal
$W\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times d}$ such that
\[
\max_i\;\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\;\le\;
\frac{25\sqrt{d}\;n\,\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}\,.
\]
\end{lemma}
\begin{proof}
Entrywise, $|D^2-D^{*2}|\le4\varepsilon_\infty$, and double centering inflates an
entrywise bound by at most a factor of $4$, so $E=B-B^*$ satisfies
$\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le\|E\|_F\le8\,n\,\varepsilon_\infty$. Let $B_d$ be the top-$d$
truncation of $B$ that classical MDS embeds ($B_d=\hat\Psi\hat\Psi^\top$, positive
semidefinite). By Weyl's inequality every eigenvalue of $B$ beyond the $d$-th, and every
negative eigenvalue, has magnitude at most $\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$ (since
$\lambda_{d+1}(B^*)=0$ and $B^*\succeq0$), so
$\|B_d-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}\le\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}$. The matrix $B_d-B^*$ has rank at most
$2d$, hence
\[
\|B_d-B^*\|_F\;\le\;\sqrt{2d}\,\|B_d-B^*\|_{\mathrm{op}}
\;\le\;\sqrt{2d}\,\big(\|B_d-B\|_{\mathrm{op}}+\|E\|_{\mathrm{op}}\big)
\;\le\;2\sqrt{2d}\,\|E\|_F .
\]
Both $B_d=\hat\Psi\hat\Psi^\top$ and $B^*=\Psi^*\Psi^{*\top}$ are positive semidefinite of
rank at most $d$, so by the Procrustes alignment lemma of
\citet[Lemma~5.4]{tu2016procrustes},
\[
\min_{W\in O(d)}\;\|\hat\Psi-\Psi^*W\|_F^2
\;\le\;\frac{\|B_d-B^*\|_F^2}{2(\sqrt2-1)\,\lambda_d(B^*)}\,.
\]
Combining, $\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le\|\hat\Psi-\Psi^*W\|_F\le
\frac{2\sqrt{2d}}{\sqrt{2(\sqrt2-1)}}\cdot\frac{8n\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}
\le\frac{25\sqrt d\,n\varepsilon_\infty}{\sqrt{\lambda_d(B^*)}}$. Nearest-neighbor
distances are invariant to $W$.
\end{proof}

\paragraph{Proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:qeff}.} Take scores in $[0,1]$ without loss of
generality (A1). Fix $\epsilon>0$ and set targets $c\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}/(8\gamma)$ for
the estimation error and $\epsilon'\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}/(2\gamma)$ for the population
nearest-neighbor distance. By (A2) there is $n_0(\epsilon')$ such that for $n\ge n_0$, a
reference model within population distance $\epsilon'$ of the target exists except with
probability at most $\epsilon/8$. Fix such an $n$. Applying Lemma~\ref{lem:conc} with
confidence $\delta=\epsilon/(8n^2)$ to every pair, a union bound and
Lemma~\ref{lem:pert} give $\max_i\|\hat\psi_i-W\psi_i\|\le c$ except with probability at
most $\epsilon/8$, once
\[
m\;\ge\;C'\,\frac{d\,(1+B_R)^2\,\bar B^2\,n^2\log(n/\epsilon)}{\kappa^4\,\lambda_d\,c^2}
\;=\;\mathrm{poly}(n,1/\kappa,1/\epsilon),
\]
for an absolute constant $C'$ (this choice also satisfies the proviso
$t_m\le\kappa/2$ of Lemma~\ref{lem:conc}), and does not involve the pairing fraction
$\rho$. On the intersection of the two
events, for a target model $f$ with nearest estimated reference neighbor $f^*$
(perspectives $\psi^*,\hat\psi^*$; the target's $\psi,\hat\psi$),
\[
|\hat y(f)-y(f)|\;\le\;\gamma\,\|\psi^*-\psi\|\;\le\;
\gamma\big(\|\hat\psi^*-W\psi^*\|+\|\hat\psi^*-\hat\psi\|+\|\hat\psi-W\psi\|\big)
\;\le\;\gamma\,(2c+\hat\delta),
\]
using (A1) and orthogonal invariance. The estimated nearest-neighbor distance
$\hat\delta$ is at most the estimated distance to the $\epsilon'$-close reference, itself
at most $\epsilon'+2c$ by the same alignment argument, so
$|\hat y-y|\le\gamma(4c+\epsilon')\le\sqrt{\epsilon/2}$. Squaring, and adding the two
failure events (squared errors are bounded by $1$),
$\mathrm{MSE}(\hat y)\le\epsilon/2+\epsilon/4\le\epsilon$. \hfill$\qed$

\paragraph{Contrast with paired DKPS.} With $k_Q=\delta$, only identically-shared query
pairs enter the double sum, so the $U$-statistic runs over the $\rho M_{ij}$ shared queries
and the rate in Lemma~\ref{lem:conc} becomes $O_P\big((\rho M_{ij})^{-1/2}\big)$: DKPS's
guarantee requires $\rho M_{ij}\to\infty$ and vacates in the unpaired limit $\rho\to0$,
where its distance is undefined (Proposition~\ref{prop:gen}). This asymmetry motivates
Conjecture~\ref{conj:qeff}; a formal lower bound for DKPS is future work.
